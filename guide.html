Part I — Using it

Quick start

  • New here? Press Start here (by the Guide link) for a five-step walk round the page, then try one of today's lessons at the top of the Explore tab: they're found in this forecast.
  • Explore a question. The top of the panel sets everything up to answer one question, such as "The jet stream" or "How unusual is it?". Its card says what to look for, a live number from the data, and links here. Change anything and it becomes Custom.
  • View. The row under the model switch: OPC chart (straight down on OPC's chart area), Whole (20°S–65°N), Oblique, Low pass and Globe. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom, right-drag to pan.
  • Layers. The table lists the atmosphere's levels top to bottom with their real heights. Pick one focus level to colour, with particles or barbs; tick lines on others to show their contours as context, and H/L to label their highs and lows.
  • Time. The page opens at now. The slider runs from 24 hours ago (with real satellite images) to 7 days ahead. Markers show each model run's start, the last satellite image, and now; Now jumps back. Space plays, ← → step between real times. The OPC chart menu beside it lays a forecasters' chart (OPC or HFO) on the map at its valid time (orange ticks; ◀ ▶ jump between them).
  • Getting oriented. Lat/lon lines every 10° with the equator in red, place names, a compass (top right) and a height ruler standing on the map in 3-D views.
  • Hover anywhere for the values at that point, from the real model step nearest the time shown. Click (without dragging) to pin a probe: that column's profile now, and a 7-day meteogram.
  • Heights are given in km and feet: the level table shows both; the ruler, the slice's axis, the probe and the hover readout use one unit, switched by clicking the table's height header, U, or "Heights in" on the Map tab (1 m = 3.28084 ft; feet rounded to 100).
  • Keys: U km/feet, H (or the Hide ✕ button) hides the controls, M flips between GFS and ECMWF, B blinks the model's colour fill off and on (to compare with the satellite under it), C blinks a forecasters' chart, [ ] step the slice.

Your first ten minutes

A tour that touches most of what the explorer can do, in an order that builds up. Each step says what you should see, so you can tell it's working and start to read the weather.

  1. The big picture. The explorer opens on it, at "now": the forecaster's two workhorse maps at once, in an oblique 3-D view. Below, the sea-level isobars with their H and L and the 10 m wind as moving particles; above, see-through, the 500 mb heights with their relief stretched ×4 so troughs dip and ridges rise, coloured by wind speed so the jet stream's fast cores glow, with their own H and L. Find a deep trough at 500 mb and look for the surface low under its downwind (eastern) side, where the air aloft spreads and rises; then press Play and watch both move east together. (Why not particles or flow lines at 500 mb too? The wind there runs along the height lines, so flow lines would repeat them, and a second set of particles would hide the surface's; the colour shows what the lines can't: where the jet is fastest.)
  2. Read the labels. The surface's H and L are sea-level pressure in hPa (hectopascals, the same as millibars), on isobars, lines of equal pressure. The 500 mb level's are different: that map is a surface of equal pressure, so it shows how high 500 mb is found, in decametres (tens of metres). "L 531·500" means the 500 mb surface is 531 dam (5,310 m) up at that low; its lines are height contours (isohypses), every 6 dam. Cold, dense air shrinks the column under it, so the 500 mb surface sits low over cold air: low heights are troughs of cold air; warm air expands and lifts it: high heights are ridges of warm air. (A typical 500 mb height is about 570 dam over warm subtropical air and 510–530 dam in a deep winter trough.)
  3. Where's the weather now? Pick it under Explore. You're looking straight down at the North Pacific with the newest satellite image under the sea-level isobars. Find the biggest white swirl of cloud: there's almost always a red L under or just behind its head. The swirl is the storm's cloud shield; the isobars circle the low.
  4. Hover over the low's centre, then over the open ocean far from it. The readout's pressure difference (often 20–40 hPa) is what drives the wind between them.
  5. Press Play (Space). The first part of the day is observed satellite with the models' past hours; after the satellite ends marker it's forecast only. Watch the lows travel east and the cloud with them. Press Now to come back.
  6. Lows, highs and the jet above them. The 250 mb wind is coloured; the surface isobars and 500 mb heights are lines. Switch the view to Oblique: the three levels are stacked at their true heights (stretched ×120). The jet stream is the ribbon of red and purple, 10 km up. Notice where the surface lows sit relative to it: usually on its poleward side, often under its exit.
  7. Click on a low to pin the probe. The profile shows temperature (red) and dewpoint (green) up the column: where the two lines touch, the air is saturated (cloud). The meteogram shows the pressure at that point over the next week. Tick both models: where GFS and ECMWF part company, the forecast is uncertain.
  8. What steers the storms. Now 500 mb is coloured by height and stretched ×5 so its troughs and ridges show as valleys and hills. Play and follow a surface low: it drifts with the 500 mb flow, at about half its speed.
  9. Slice. On the 3-D tab, set Shape to a vertical wall, tick Plane, keep "north–south" and slide it to 170°E. The flat chart shows the atmosphere as a cross-section: the jet is a red bullseye near the top around 35–45°N, and the θ lines (potential temperature) crowd into a sloping band beneath it. That band is the boundary between polar and tropical air.
  10. How unusual is it? The 500 mb height minus its 1991–2020 normal for this date and hour. Blue blobs are colder, lower air than usual; red, warmer and higher. Big anomalies are the week's story.
  11. Compare with the forecasters. Pick OPC surface by the timeline. OPC's own hand-drawn analysis appears under the model's isobars (now magenta). Do the lows match? The fronts are only on OPC's chart: look for where the model's isobars kink.
  12. Globe. Everything bends onto the Earth. The Mercator map stretched the Aleutians and the Bering Sea; on the globe they're their true size.

Exploring in 3-D: a walk-through

The 3-D tab is where you look into the atmosphere rather than at one level of it. It works in three steps, always in the same order: where (a region), what (one quantity), and how (any mix of ways of drawing it). This walk-through takes about ten minutes and uses whatever the weather is today.

  1. Clear the stage. On the Layers tab keep only the surface (isobars) so the scene is quiet. On the Map tab set Map and lat/lon lines on to the surface. (The background turns light grey by itself when you turn on a colour fill: see-through fills show about twice as clearly on it as on black.)
  2. Where: a box round a storm. 3-D tab → Shape → a box. Drag its white middle handle onto the biggest surface low (the red L), and its yellow corners out until the box holds the low and its fronts, roughly 20° of longitude by 15° of latitude. Set Thickness 6 km and Bottom 1 km. The white outline shows the box; everything else fades outside it.
  3. What: vorticity. Show in the region → quantity vorticity. The line under the menu says what it means: the air's spin.
  4. How: a colour fill. Tick Colour fill, only beyond a threshold at 8. Red shapes appear where the air spins like a low: usually a column over the low, leaning west with height toward the trough aloft, and a thin sheet along the cold front. Press Orbit it and drag to walk round it.
  5. Add the flow. Tick 3-D flow lines, coloured by the quantity, with Rise and sink at ×5. The lines spiral in round the red column low down and fan out higher up; the ones that climb steeply are the storm's warm conveyor belt, rising over the warm front.
  6. Change the question, keep the view. Switch the quantity to θe, then frontogenesis, then rising / sinking air. The box, camera and flow lines stay; only the colours' meaning changes. Each tells a piece of the same storm (see spin, spreading, turbulence and stability).
  7. Take a slice. Switch the shape to a vertical wall through the low and tick Plane: the flat cross-section chart shows the same storm as a classic textbook cross-section.
  8. Share it. Share tab → Copy link to this view: the region, quantity, fills, flow lines and camera all go in the link.
Tips. Lower the vertical stretch (Layers → Shape) to ×40–60 when you orbit a box: the storm looks less like a skyscraper and the lean with height reads more honestly. Use From below to stand under the storm and look up through the fill. If the scene gets busy, drop the fill's opacity to 0.3–0.5 and keep only one of particles or flow lines.

Every control, explained

The panel has the model switch at the top (GFS or ECMWF, with its run; M flips), the row of views, and six tabs: Explore (questions), Layers (which levels, what the focus level shows and how opaque, and a fold-out Shape group: vertical stretch, relief, shading, other levels), 3-D (a region to explore: a wall, a layer or a box, and what to show inside it; see the walk-through), Map (satellite, OPC charts' availability; the map and where it and the lat/lon lines go, the background, place names, OPC area, ruler, H and L on/off, height units), Style (each level's line colour and width, particle and barb looks; your choices are remembered on this device, and reset restores them) and Share (tours, and saving the view). Tapping the open tab again folds the panel down to its tabs, to see more of the map. The last tab used is remembered on this device. The views (OPC chart, Whole, Oblique, Low pass, Globe) are a row at the top of the panel, above the tabs, so they work from any tab; with the panel hidden they sit beside ☰ Controls. The page's header is two short lines; About opens the full notes and credits.

GFS − ECMWF: where the models differ

Under Colour by, as GFS − ECMWF turns the focus level's colour into the difference between the two models at the same valid time: red where GFS is higher, blue where ECMWF is, white where they agree. It works for height, temperature, wind speed, humidity, vertical motion, sea-level pressure, 2 m temperature, water vapour, sea temperature and waves (not for anomalies, whose difference is the same as the fields', derived quantities or observed sea temperature). The sign is always GFS minus ECMWF, whichever model is on screen; the lines, wind and labels stay the on-screen model's. Hover gives the difference with both values ("GFS − ECMWF, 500 mb height: −13 m (GFS 5451, ECMWF 5464)"). The colour range is fixed per quantity (±60 m for heights, ±4 °C, ±20 kt, ±8 hPa, ±10 mm, ±2 m for waves), so a colour means the same difference at every time; bigger differences read out true.

  • Each model uses its own run. This build's newest runs start at different times (GFS 18Z, ECMWF 12Z, say), so part of any difference is that one forecast is six hours older. The note above the map names both runs. Near the end of the week one model's run ends first; there the colour stops, and the note says why.
  • It grows with time. On a real build: the sea-level pressure difference averaged 0.6 hPa over the map now and for the past 12 hours, 0.6 at +24 h, 1.1 at +72 h and 1.4 hPa at +120 h; the share of the map where the models differ by 4 hPa or more went from 0.1 % to 3.8 % to 8.7 %. The 500 mb height difference averaged 6–7 m now, 10 m at +24 h, 14 m at +72 h and 16 m at +120 h. That's forecast uncertainty, made visible: small errors in the starting state grow.
  • What to look for: a red–blue pair side by side around a low means the two models put the low in different places (red where GFS has higher pressure, i.e. where GFS doesn't have the low); a single blue blob means ECMWF has it deeper. Differences aren't errors of one model: until the time comes, neither is known to be right.

In 3-D. On the 3-D tab, as GFS − ECMWF under the region's quantity does the same for the colour fill and the plane (and a wall's slice): the difference is taken level by level at the same valid time, then filled between the levels on the on-screen model's level heights like any quantity. Particles and flow lines stay one model's wind. With the fill as a shape, it shows only the air where the models differ by at least the threshold: on a real build, the share of all the air up to 12.5 km where the two models' wind speeds differ by 10 kt or more grew from 2.7 % now to 11.2 % at +72 h and 14.8 % at +120 h. Watch where it grows first: usually around the jet and the storms, where small differences in position make big differences in value. Ranges: ±20 kt, ±3 °C, ±3 K, ±30 % humidity, ±10 cm/s, and for the derived quantities about a third of their usual span.

Cases: real events, step by step

The Cases tab walks through real North Pacific events of the past year in the Past year viewer, Pacific Northwest and Hawaiʻi first: in the Northwest, atmospheric rivers, bomb cyclones (and a Fujiwhara pair), heat domes, windstorms, Arctic outbreaks and ice storms, a stuck ridge; in Hawaiʻi, Kona lows, a channel gale and the calm after it, the Lahaina wind, big surf near and far, and hurricanes; then El Niño, typhoons' second lives in Alaska, a blocked Alaskan winter and Bering Sea storms. Events from before the rolling year come from the event archive. Pick one; each step sets the map (what's shown, anomaly or value, lines and wind), opens its day and flies the camera there. The step's text has four parts: On the map, a key saying what each layer shows (the colour fill and what its colours mean, the lines, the wind and how it's drawn, the markers), written from the step's own settings so it always matches the map, the story with its numbers, Why this view (why this map answers the step's question) and Look for (what to find on it). Amber rings and short labels mark the features the text names, on the step's own day only (step to another day and they go, since the storm has moved); Mark the features on the map turns them off. Next and Back move between steps; some steps offer Play these days to run the event's days, one real 00 UTC analysis every 0.7 s, and some pin the probe to chart that place's whole year. At any step the map is yours: change the map, zoom, hover, then press Next to carry on. A share link made during a case opens the same case at the same step.

Every number in a case's text comes from the archive's own GFS analyses (the day named, 00 UTC, on its 1° grid) unless the text credits a source; each case lists its sources at the bottom. Two things the grid can't do, and the cases say so where it matters: resolve a hurricane's or typhoon's small core (it always looks weaker here: Nolo was 929 hPa to the forecasters, 979 on this grid), and show what happens between one day's analysis and the next (a surge or a windstorm peaks in hours). The archive rolls forward a day at a time, so a case whose days have left it is shown greyed out.

The forecasters' own charts. Some steps show the charts the forecasters drew at the time, as they were broadcast: the Ocean Prediction Center's Pacific surface analysis (every 6 hours, in east and west halves), its 500 mb analysis, and its 24-, 48-, 72- and 96-hour surface and 500 mb forecasts; and the Honolulu office's Pacific streamline analysis and wind-and-wave charts. A menu picks the kind and ◀ ▶ step through its times (switching kind keeps the time as near as it can); a forecast says when it was made and how far ahead; click a chart to open it full size. They come from NCEI's archive of NCEP charts, which keeps the black-and-white radiofax versions: the colour charts on the web were replaced every few hours, and no archive kept the Pacific ones for these days. The analysis is drawn by people from the observations and the models, so it's a second opinion beside the model's map: where they differ, it's worth asking why. Each chart's time was checked against its own title box. On the map (ticked by default) lays the chart in place over the 3-D view, over the case's map and under its wind, with its own opacity slider: blink it off and on to compare the forecasters' isobars with the model's. That works because these charts are Mercator, like the map: a registration tool (tools/register_case_charts.py) finds each chart's 10-degree grid lines, fits a Mercator mapping (every frame within 2.5 px, both axes agreeing on the scale within 1 %) and records where the chart's box lies in it; spot checks put Oʻahu, the Big Island, Cape Mendocino, Cape Flattery and Kodiak where the charts draw them. The 24-hour surface forecast is polar stereographic and the 72-hour one is drawn differently, so those two stay in the panel only. The Alenuihāhā case has 135 of them: analyses for 16–22 December 2023, and every forecast that was valid during the crossing.

Today's lessons

The cases teach the past; today's lessons teach the weather in this forecast. A few seconds after the page opens it looks through the forecast (the surface every 12 hours over the week, and the 250 mb wind now: about 6 MB) for features worth a lesson, and lists them at the top of the Explore tab with their numbers:

  • The week's deepest low north of 30°N: three steps, from 36 hours before its deepest (the surface low and the 500 mb trough to its west) to its deepest (how much it fell in 24 hours, and whether that makes it a "bomb": 24 hPa in 24 hours at 60°N, scaled by the sine of the latitude, after Sanders and Gyakum 1980), then the 500 mb pattern that drives it.
  • A tropical cyclone (anything below 1000 mb between 5 and 30°N): the storm, the storm in 3-D, and what steers it, with the caution that the global model's grid is too coarse for its core.
  • The fastest jet at 250 mb now, and what it does to the weather below.
  • The moistest air north of 35°N, an atmospheric river when it's 30 mm or more. Tropical cyclones' own moisture (within 1,200 km of any tropical low under 1000 mb) is left out: on this lesson's first real test a typhoon's moisture reached 30°N, 1,000 km north of its centre, and passed for a river.
  • Hawaiʻi's trades: the model's strongest wind by the Alenuihāhā Channel this week, read as a floor (the case explains why).
  • The biggest seas in the wave model.

Each step sets the map, the time and the camera (with the usual "On the map" key below), marks what it's talking about with amber rings and labels, as the cases do (shown while the time is within 3 hours of the step's, since the features move), and buttons do the next thing for you: play toward the moment, jump to it, or drop a Drop-tab tool right at the feature (forces, where the air came from, an air parcel, a lifted parcel, a weather balloon). The lessons change with every build. Start here, by the Guide link, walks you round the page in five steps (it opens by itself on a first visit).

Layers

The model (with its run, e.g. "GFS 28/00Z"), then the column. One focus level gets the colour fill and its wind, drawn any mix of three ways: particles, barbs and flow lines (see three ways to draw the wind). With the surface in focus, its particles box and this menu drive the same particles. The 3-D tab: a region to explore

The 3-D tools, a slice plane, a volume fill and particles at every height, all work inside one region. You place it once, and they all follow it (user request: they used to be separate tools with their own sliders).

  • Shape: the whole atmosphere (surface to 12.5 km, the whole map), a vertical wall (a band along a line: its run, position, turn and width), a horizontal layer (the whole map, between two heights), or a box: a latitude/longitude box between two heights, set by its south, north, west and east edges (half-degree steps, at least 2° each way) or dragged in the scene. It opens on 35–50°N, 175°E–155°W, the middle of the storm track, about 2,460 by 1,670 km. On the map a box is a rectangle, so the page treats it the same as a wide wall: the fill, particles, flow lines and fading all keep to it exactly (on a real run all 12,000 particles stayed inside). Use it to follow one storm: put the box round the low, lift it to 2–8 km, and watch the flow lines and a vorticity or θe fill inside it.
  • Outline the region: the region's edges drawn in the scene, white with a dark edge so they show on either background: a box's or a wall's band's rectangle at the bottom and the top, joined at the corners; a layer's two rectangles over the map.
  • Heights (wall, layer and box): thickness, then bottom, in km and feet with the rough pressure level ("≈500 mb"). All heights fills the column; sweep up lifts the region from the surface to the top in about 12 seconds (press again to stop), like a CT scan moving up.
  • Show in the region: first the quantity: wind speed (and, for a wall, the wind across or along it), rising and sinking air, vorticity, divergence, turbulence risk, temperature, θ, stability, θe, thermal gradient, frontogenesis, humidity, and the anomalies (see what the derived quantities mean). A line under it says what it is. Then every way of showing it, any mix, each with its own opacity so you can look through one to another:
    • Colour fill: the quantity as a see-through colour, everywhere in the region (a tint that shows its whole pattern) or only beyond a threshold (a 3-D shape: the jet as a tube, the turbulent zones as sheets).
    • Particles: always moving with the wind, whatever the quantity; the quantity (or height) only sets their colour. So with vorticity picked, the particles still show where the wind blows, and their colour shows where it spins. The same goes for the 3-D flow lines.
    • 3-D flow lines: lines that follow the wind through the region from points inside it, at the nearer real step (a snapshot of the flow, not the path air will take as the pattern moves); faint where they start, bright where they end, so you can see which way the air goes; coloured by the quantity or by height. 150–600 lines, 6–24 hours of flow each, and they can climb and sink with the vertical motion like the particles.
    • Plane: a wall's slice, or a horizontal map at a height, coloured by the quantity, with lines.
    Everything keeps inside the region: a wall's band, a box, or a layer, between its heights. Anomalies can only be a fill (the plane then shows temperature).
  • Outside the region: the other layers you have on (the level fills and lines, their particles, barbs and flow lines) stay, but fade outside it by the amount you set (35 % by default; 0 hides them there, 100 % leaves them alone), so the region stands out and you keep the context. For a wall or a box, a level fades outside its outline and heights; for a layer, the levels outside its heights fade. The levels' own particles, barbs and flow lines fade evenly. It applies only while something is shown in the region.
  • Widths are measured on the map (the Mercator map the page is drawn on), so a band is narrower on the ground toward the north: a 300 km band is about 190 km across on the ground at 50°N. A thin slice gives the volume and particles a 300 km band.

What the derived quantities mean

These are worked out on the page from each level's wind, temperature, humidity and height (so they need no extra data), then filled between the levels like everything else. Ranges below are from a real GFS run.

  • Vorticity (×10⁻⁵ per second): the air's spin about the vertical, ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y, on the sphere. Red is counter-clockwise, which north of the equator is cyclonic, turning like a low; blue clockwise, anticyclonic. South of the equator (the data reaches 20°S) lows turn clockwise, so there a low shows blue. About ±8 to 19 at 850–250 mb. Look for: red blobs in troughs and lows, and red/blue stripes along the jet's cold and warm flanks. Air carries its spin with it, so a red blob moving east ahead of a trough often means a surface low is about to deepen under it.
  • Divergence (×10⁻⁵ per second): ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y. Red where air spreads out, blue where it piles in. About ±6 to 7. Look for: blue (converging) low down and red (spreading) aloft over the same place, which squeezes air upward: that's where rising air, cloud and rain are (compare with rising/sinking air). At the surface, blue lines of convergence mark the ITCZ, fronts and storm inflow.
  • Turbulence risk (Ellrod's index, ×10⁻⁷ per second²): vertical wind shear × deformation (how much the wind is stretching and shearing the air). The clear-air-turbulence index aviation forecasters use: about 4 light to moderate, 8 moderate, 12 and up severe. Most of the atmosphere is below 1; up to 20 near the jet at 250 mb. Look for: sheets above and below the jet core, and around deep lows. It's a risk index, not an observation of bumps.
  • Stability (dθ/dz, K per km): how fast potential temperature rises with height. 3 to 5.5 in the ordinary troposphere; above 10 in inversions and above the tropopause (the stratosphere is very stable); near 0 where air can overturn. Look for: the tropopause as a sharp jump, lower over the Arctic than the tropics, and dipping under troughs.
  • θe, equivalent potential temperature (K; Bolton's formula): θ with the heat the air's water vapour would release when it condenses counted in, so it marks air masses by both warmth and moisture. 282 to 358 K. Look for: tongues of high θe (warm, moist tropical air) reaching north ahead of storms, and the sharp edge where they meet cold, dry air: a front. Where θe falls with height, the air can turn stormy when lifted.
  • Thermal gradient (|∇θ|, K per 100 km): how fast temperature changes across the map at that height. The NWS Unified Surface Analysis Manual defines a front as a band of strong gradient, about 6 °C over 500 km, which is 1.2 here. Up to about 4.5. Look for: the front zones as bands, and in 3-D how they lean back over the cold air with height.
  • Frontogenesis (K per 100 km per 3 hours): whether the wind is sharpening the temperature gradient (red: a front forming or strengthening) or smoothing it out (blue: weakening). About ±1.5 to 2. Look for: red along the fronts of developing storms, blue where old fronts are dying.

The last three come from the sibling OPC project's front diagnostics, which draw them at 850 mb; here they're at every level. Its automatic front lines aren't used (the fields show fronts continuously instead).

How much of the air passes each default threshold (a real GFS run, all the air to 12.5 km over the map): turbulence index ≥ 8: 1.4 %; |vorticity| ≥ 8: 4.8 %; |divergence| ≥ 4: 4.0 %; stability ≥ 8 K/km: 0.9 % (below the tropopause's full depth, which mostly lies above 12.5 km in the tropics); θe ≥ 330 K: 50 %; thermal gradient ≥ 1.2: 17 %; |frontogenesis| ≥ 1: 2.5 %. So the default shapes pick out the few percent of the air where something is happening, except θe, which splits the air roughly in half (tropical against polar). For the science behind them, see spin, spreading, turbulence and stability.

The horizontal plane (3-D tab)

With a layer, a box or the whole atmosphere as the region, Plane draws a horizontal map at one height, set with At (kept inside the region's heights; shown in km, feet and rough mb). It shows the region's quantity (wind speed, temperature, θ, humidity, vertical motion or any of the derived quantities) at exactly that height, filled between the model's levels the same way as the slice, blended between the two real steps, and it rises and stretches with the vertical stretch like the levels. In a box it stops at the box's edges (the other levels only fade there; the plane is part of the region, so it's cut off). It has its own flat map chart (the nearer real step, with coastlines; hover for values and the pressure there).

  • Lines: pressure every 4 hPa makes it a constant-height chart, the other way of mapping the upper air. Weather charts usually show the height of a pressure level (500 mb heights); this shows the pressure at a height. The two tell the same story: where the 500 mb surface dips (a trough), the pressure at 5.5 km is low. At 5.5 km the pressure ranges from about 490 hPa in cold northern air to 525 hPa over the warm tropics, because warm air is less dense, so pressure falls off more slowly with height through it.
  • Try it: a layer from the surface to 12.5 km, the plane at 1.5 km coloured by temperature, then drag At up slowly: the warm and cold air masses shift and fade with height, the lows' closed isobars open into troughs aloft, and near 10 km the jet appears as a band of fast wind.

Dragging the region in the scene (3-D tab)

On the flat map the region has handles you can grab with the mouse (turn them off with Drag handles in the scene). Grabbing one holds the camera still; the panel's sliders follow as you drag.

  • White (a wall's or a box's centre): slide the wall across the map in half-degree steps; move a box anywhere, keeping its size, until it meets the map's edge.
  • Yellow (a wall's end): turn it, up to 90° either way, in 1° steps. A box has two yellow handles on opposite corners (south-west and north-east): drag them to resize it.
  • Blue (bottom and top, on either side of the centre so they don't overlap seen from above): drag up or down on the screen to lower or raise that height, in quarter-km steps. The rate is the screen distance a km of height covers at the handle, but at least 20 pixels per km, so looking straight down (where height has no direction on the screen) a drag still moves it steadily.
  • Green (the horizontal plane): raise or lower the plane, inside the region's heights.

Views of the region (3-D tab)

View it flies the camera to a view of the region; afterwards drag to look around it. All are on the flat map.

  • From above: straight down onto the region from directly overhead, framed to fit it: the fill, particles and plane seen as a map. (For the whole data area from overhead, use Whole in the View row.)
  • Face: square onto a wall (its whole length in view), or looking down at an angle onto a layer.
  • Edge-on: along a wall's line, so its band's thickness fills the view; or level with a layer from the south, so you see it as a slab of air.
  • Orbit it: the camera's pivot moves to the region's centre (its middle height), so dragging spins you around it rather than around the map.
  • From below: standing at the surface under the region, looking up (about 40–55°) into it: particles stream overhead, the volume hangs above you.
  • Inside: at the region's middle height, looking along it: flying through the slab.

Turn off all 3-D (top of the 3-D tab) unticks the colour fill, particles, flow lines and plane at once and sets the region back to the whole atmosphere, so its outline, handles and the fading of other layers go too: back to the plain map and its levels, with the camera left where it is.

Remember the stretch. Heights are stretched (×120 by default) so the atmosphere's 12 km show against a 10,000 km map; from below or inside, what looks like a tall wall of air is really a thin film. Lower the vertical stretch (Layers tab) to see closer to true proportions. From below or inside the camera can look up, which the map views don't allow; picking a map view (the View row at the top) puts that back.

Slice (a vertical cross-section, like a CT scan)

A vertical sheet standing in the scene along a line on the map, from the surface to 12.5 km. It's built from all seven levels of the model on screen at the two real steps around the time shown, and blended like everything else.

  • Run north–south (at a longitude) or east–west (at a latitude); Position slides it across the map ([ ] step it 2°, sweep runs it across); Turn rotates it about its middle.
  • Thickness: a thin slice, or a slab 250–1,000 km wide, averaged across five parallel lines (like a slab in a CT scan); it smooths small features and shows the broad structure.
  • Colour: the region's quantity. For a wall there are two extra: wind across the slice (red = through it from its left to its right, blue = the other way: jet cores show as bullseyes) and wind along it. The rest are wind speed, θ, temperature, humidity, vertical motion (rising blue, sinking brown) and the derived quantities: a slice coloured by stability shows the tropopause as a sharp step, by turbulence risk the sheets above and below the jet, by θe the air masses meeting at a front. Lines: θ every 4 K, or wind every 20 kt.
  • Motion arrows (flat chart): how the air moves in the slice's plane, combining the wind along the slice and the vertical motion. The chart stretches height a lot (the info line says by how much, often ×100 or more) and the arrows are stretched the same way, so each slopes exactly as the air moves across that picture. They have a fixed length (direction only); the colour shows strength. See rising and sinking air.
  • Pressure levels on the slice (on by default): each level drawn across the slice at its real height, in 3-D and on the flat chart alike, in the level's colour with the focus level bold and a dark outline, and its name at the far end. The levels sag over cold air and troughs and rise over warm air and ridges, and the gap between two is that layer's thickness. A line breaks where its level is below the surface: under mountains, and also for 1000 mb under a low whose surface pressure is below 1000 hPa (the "1000 mb surface" would be under the sea there). From straight above the names all land on one spot and only one shows; face the slice (or zoom in) to see them all.
  • Face it turns the camera square onto the slice; Square up straightens and centres it.
  • Flat chart (on by default): the same slice as a classic cross-section plot beside the panel, with height up (km or feet), position across (latitude or longitude) and contour lines. Each pressure level is drawn as a line at its real height along the slice, in the level's own colour, with the focus level bold and its name at the right: you see the pressure surfaces sag over cold air and troughs and rise over warm air and ridges, and the gap between two lines is that layer's thickness (its temperature; see thickness). A line breaks where its level is below ground. It shows the nearer real model step, never a blend, and names it; hover for values. In 3-D, faced square-on across the whole domain the slice is only about 1:8 tall; the chart has its own shape.
What to look for. Along 180°: the jet core (a red bullseye near 250 mb around 35–45°N), θ lines crowding into a sloping band under it (the front, cold air to the north), and θ lines packed tight up high where the stratosphere begins (the tropopause), much higher over the tropics than the Arctic. Near the equator: tall moist columns (the ITCZ) and weak winds.

Colour fill (3-D tab)

The region's quantity as a see-through colour in 3-D, at any time on the slider (the past 24 hours from the earlier runs' steps, and the forecast). Every grid column is filled between the model's levels the same way as the slice, on 48 heights, for the two real steps around the time shown, and blended. It's drawn only inside the region.

  • Everywhere, see-through: the whole region tinted by the quantity. Good for a smooth quantity whose whole pattern matters: temperature, θ, θe, humidity. Seen from the side you get the air masses stacked in 3-D.
  • Only beyond a threshold (a shape): just the air past the threshold, so the rest is empty and the shape stands out. For quantities with a sign (vorticity, divergence, rising/sinking, frontogenesis, the anomalies) both ends show: red beyond +threshold, blue beyond −threshold. Defaults: wind ≥ 70 kt (the jet as a 3-D river; 100 kt for just its core), RH ≥ 80 % (humid air, not the same as cloud), rising or sinking ≥ 8 cm/s (about 290 m an hour), vorticity and divergence beyond ±8 and ±4 ×10⁻⁵/s, turbulence index ≥ 8 (moderate), stability ≥ 8 K/km, θe ≥ 330 K, thermal gradient ≥ 1.2 K/100 km (the NWS front threshold), frontogenesis beyond ±1, anomalies beyond ±3 °C and ±100 m. Every threshold can go down to show everything.
  • The header says what share of all the air up to 12.5 km over the map passes the threshold, so you know whether you're looking at a rare feature (1–5 %) or half the atmosphere.
What to look for. A cold trough shows (temperature anomaly) as a blue column leaning west with height, a blocking ridge as a tall red dome. The jet at ≥ 100 kt is a tube a few km deep along 35–45°N, fattest off Japan in winter. Turbulence ≥ 8 is thin sheets hugging the top and bottom of that tube. Sweep a wall across the map with the fill on and it shows as a thick 3-D slice moving through the atmosphere, like a CT scan. Tips: turn the focus level's fill and particles off, and use the light background. The shape depends on the threshold you choose: move it and the shape grows or shrinks, so name the threshold when you show it to someone.

Particles (3-D tab)

Wind particles at every height at once, inside the region: a layer (set its thickness, then its bottom, 0 = the surface, and move it up through the atmosphere like a CT scan), a wall's band or a box (they start in it and start again when the wind carries them out), or the whole atmosphere. Sweep up lifts the region from the surface to the top in about 12 seconds (press again to stop); all heights shows the whole depth. The slab's edges are given in km and feet (the height ⇄ unit first) and as a rough pressure, "≈500 mb": the standard atmosphere's pressure at that height (p = 1013.25 (1 − z / 44,331 m)5.256), rounded to 5 mb. It tells you which of the model's levels the slab holds; on the day, a level sits a few hundred metres higher in warm air and lower in cold air, so the real 500 mb surface may be at 5.2 km in a deep trough and 5.9 km under a ridge. Each particle keeps its own altitude and drifts with the horizontal wind there, filled between the model's levels the same way as the slice, at the two real steps around the time shown. The column starts with the 10 m wind, 10 m above the ground, so a slab from the surface includes the winds right at the sea surface. Colour by height (the levels' own colours, blended between them, so a particle at 5.5 km has 500 mb's colour) or by the quantity (the region's: wind speed, vertical motion, vorticity, θe and so on, with its colour key; anomalies colour by height, since they're a fill only). Rise and sink lets each particle also climb or sink with the model's vertical motion where it is: at true speed it climbs in the same flow time as it drifts, so its path has the air's real slope (then stretched by the vertical stretch like everything else); ×5 exaggerates the climb itself so weak motion shows, and the page says so. A particle that reaches the ground or the top starts again elsewhere.

What to look for. Slide a thin slab (1–2 km) up from the surface: the winds turn clockwise and strengthen with height over a warm-air advection zone, the swirl around a low tightens or fades, and around 9–11 km the jet appears as a fast river. With all heights and colour by height, the stack shows at a glance how the wind changes direction between the surface and the jet. Turn on rise and sink with colour by vertical motion and watch a storm: air ahead of the low (east of it) climbs in a broad blue sheet, and air behind the cold front sinks.

3-D flow lines (3-D tab)

Lines that follow the wind through the region in 3-D. Each starts at a random point inside the region (150, 300 or 600 of them, the same points every time for the same settings) and is traced forward through the wind of the nearer real model step, half an hour at a time, for 6, 12 or 24 hours of flow. With Rise and sink they also climb and sink with the model's vertical motion (true speed, or ×5 so weak motion shows). A line stops where it leaves the region or its heights. Each is faint where it starts and bright where it ends, so you can see which way the air goes, and coloured by the quantity along it or by height.

  • They're a snapshot. The wind is held still at one real step while the line is traced, so a line is a streamline of that moment, not the path a parcel will really take (the pattern moves while the air does). For a slow pattern the two are close; for a fast-moving storm they aren't. Step the time and the lines redraw.
  • Why use them rather than particles: particles show motion; lines show the whole pattern at once and hold still for discussion, a screenshot or a slide.
What to look for. Around a low at 1–3 km: lines spiral inward and, with rise and sink, climb. Around a high: they spiral out and sink. In a box through the jet, coloured by speed: straight, fast, parallel lines, bunching where the jet accelerates. Coloured by θe with rise and sink ×5 in a storm: warm, moist (orange) air climbing north-east over the warm front, the warm conveyor belt, and cold, dry (blue) air sinking in behind the cold front, the dry intrusion: the two streams that shape a storm's comma cloud.

How the layers are drawn

Each pressure level is a surface in the air, drawn at its real height (geopotential height × the vertical exaggeration). Its bumps are the ridges and troughs, stretched, and its height contours are drawn on it. Its wind particles and barbs ride on that surface, at the level's real height. Sea-level pressure is different: it's a pressure at a fixed height (sea level), so the isobars lie on a flat sheet just above the ground. So the surface chart is flat, and the 500 mb "chart" is a bumpy sheet about 5.5 km up (×120 on screen). The same contours seen from straight above (OPC chart view) read like the familiar upper-air chart.

Seeing a level's shape: relief, height colours, shading

A level's own bumps are small. On the real build, 500 mb spans about 5,310–5,940 m: 624 m, which at ×120 is only 75 km of relief across a 12,245 km map (0.6 %), so the sheet looks flat. Three tools show its shape:

  • Relief (×1–×10, focus level only) stretches each point's departure from the level's mean height: drawn height = mean + (height − mean) × relief. The mean stays at its true height, so the level keeps its place between its neighbours (above about ×8 a level can reach them). Troughs become valleys, ridges hills. Everything riding on the sheet moves with it: particles, barbs, lat/lon lines, labels. Readouts, H and L, the probe and the contours still use the true heights, and the header says "relief ×N … shape, not true height" whenever it's on.
  • Colour by height: low heights (troughs) blue to high (ridges) orange, the coloured 500 mb chart convention.
  • Shaded relief (on by default): filled levels are lit from the north-west, about 40° up, like a hillshade on a terrain map. It uses a copy of the surface with slopes steepened 10× (the terrain-map "z-factor"), so shapes read even at relief ×1. It's lighting only: a flat sheet keeps its exact colours.
  • Other levels can be shown as lines + faint sheet, a see-through grey, to see the stack without clutter.

H and L

Highs (blue H) and lows (red L) at the nearer real model step, on the levels ticked in the level table's H/L column: the focus level by default (labels on every level at once crowded the view). Sea-level pressure is labelled in hPa ("L 979"); levels aloft in decametres of height with the level ("L 529·500" = 5,290 m at 500 mb), the chart convention. Method in what the browser calculates.

  • Profile: at the real step nearest the time shown, a small sounding (temperature red, dewpoint green, against pressure) and a table of height, temperature, dewpoint, humidity, wind and vertical motion (w, cm/s: blue + rising, orange − sinking) by level. The Drop tab: marbles, balloons, where from?, smoke and rings

Hands-on ways to feel the shape of the air and watch it move. Pick a tool, then click the map; a card under the tools says what to try. All of them follow the focus level (Layers tab). clear removes everything; up to eight things at a time (a smoke source or a ring counts as one).

  • The marble rolls downhill on the focus level's surface (the heights, or sea-level pressure at the surface), slowed by a little friction: it runs into the troughs, swings along them and settles in the lows, so you can feel the shape that's hard to see in contour lines. With troughs & ridges only ticked (the default) it rolls on what's left once each latitude's average height is taken out: at 500 mb the heights fall steadily toward the pole, and on that slope a marble would simply run north. (Meteorologists call these troughs and ridges "waves" in the westerlies; nothing to do with ocean waves.) Drop several and watch them gather in the same trough. To compare, untick it: the marbles then roll on the raw field, and run toward the pole at 500 mb, or into the low-pressure belts at the surface (where the slope is gentler, so the two views differ less). It shows shape, not motion: real air doesn't roll downhill; turned by the Earth's spin, it runs along the height lines.
  • The balloon is that motion. It's a constant-level balloon, like the ones scientists fly for weeks at one pressure level (a party balloon would just rise until it burst): it floats on the focus level, drifts with that level's wind and leaves an orange trail. Press Play and it travels through time: each frame it moves by exactly the model hours the timeline advanced, in the wind blended between the two real model steps on either side (the same blend as the interpolated frames), so its trail is a trajectory through the changing weather. Paused, it drifts in the frozen wind of the step on screen, 2 hours of model time a second, to explore that moment; stepping the time back doesn't move it. Its limits: it stays on its pressure level (real air rises and sinks), it sees the 0.5° grid and 3-hourly steps (no gusts, no small eddies), and the forecast's errors are its errors. The marble has no clock: its speed is made up, so it shows the shape of the surface on screen, not where anything goes. The trail is one clear track among the particles, barbs and flow lines. The balloon's label gives its wind and how long it has flown. Drop one on each side of a jet, or upwind of a low.
  • The air parcel (teal) is the air itself, in 3-D (the balloon's 3-D mode until 2026-10-09: renamed, since a real balloon rises). For smoke, rings and where-from, stay on the focus level (ticked by default) keeps them on the focus level; untick it for 3-D. An air parcel starts at the focus level's real height there, then rises and sinks with the model's vertical motion, carried by the wind at whatever height it has reached (filled between the model's levels, as the 3-D particles are). This is how trajectory models such as NOAA's HYSPLIT trace where air goes. It's drawn at its true height, so it can float above or below an exaggerated relief, and its label adds its height and an arrow: ↑ rising, ↓ sinking. It stops at the ground and at the top level. Drop one of each at the same spot and press Play: ahead of a trough the air parcel climbs into faster wind, while under a ridge it sinks and slows, and the two tracks part. On one test run, two dropped together at 5.6 km, 45°N 175°W, were 16° of latitude apart after four days. Rising air is in cm/s (5 cm/s is 180 m an hour), so the climbs take a day or more.
  • Where did this air come from? Click a point: the blue line is the path of the air arriving there at the time on screen, traced backward hour by hour through the wind of each earlier hour (blended between the real steps, like the balloon), with a dot every 24 h and a label at the far end saying how long before, and at what height in 3-D. Each hour is a two-stage step: back along the wind here, then again along the average of the wind here and where that first step landed (Heun's method, which NOAA's HYSPLIT trajectories also use). Trace back sets how far: 24 h to 7 days. The data starts 24 h before "now", so from "now" you get one day at most; move the time into the forecast to trace further (the forecast hours before it count as data too). Untick stay on the focus level for a 3-D path: air arriving at 500 mb over a high often came down from higher up (sinking air warms and dries, which is why highs are clear). The path doesn't change as the time moves; it belongs to the time you dropped it. Forecasters ask this question every day: is the moist air over Hawaiʻi tropical? Did the cold air come off Siberia?
    Three heights. Set arrive to at three heights and one click traces the air arriving 500 m, 1.5 km and 5.5 km above the ground (always in 3-D), in blue, green and violet: the view NOAA's HYSPLIT gives by default. Wind changes with height, so the three often come from very different places.
    The journey chart (in the Drop tab, for the latest trace) plots each path's height, temperature and humidity hour by hour, oldest on the left. Air that sank warmed and dried (sinking air is squeezed and heats up, about 10 °C per km when dry), which is why highs are clear and why the trade-wind inversion caps the clouds; air that rose cooled and moistened toward cloud. In one test trace, air arriving 500 m up north-west of Hawaiʻi had come down from 4.1 km in 66 hours, warming from −9 to 20 °C, its humidity rising from 19 to 77 % only as it joined the moist air near the sea. Temperature and humidity are the model's, at the path's height and hour.
  • Where's this swell from? Click a spot at sea, say off a north shore: the swell arriving there at the time on screen (the wave model's peak period and direction) is traced backward. In deep water swell travels on a great circle at its group speed, g × period ÷ 4π, so each period at its own speed: 14 s swell about 11 m/s (21 kt, 940 km a day), 8 s swell about 6 m/s. So the path back is fixed by the arrival: the great circle toward where it comes from, with a dot every day. The wave model gives the seas at each point at its own earlier time, and the biggest (the red ball, "its storm?") are most likely where the swell was made. In one test the 11 s north-west swell on Oʻahu's north shore went back 6 days to 4 m seas near 47°N 159°E, off the Kuril Islands. A first version steered by the wave model's local direction, and an 11 s trade swell from the east captured it, so the great circle alone it is, as swell forecasters trace it. From "now" the data reach back only 24 h; move the time forward to trace days back (trace back sets how far). Near the coast, the wave model's land points are skipped, so a spot just off a shore still works. The biggest-seas lesson has a button for Oʻahu's north shore.
  • Smoke. A source that lets go of a puff every model hour (on the model's clock: while playing, or 2 h a second when paused), each carried like a balloon, fading as it ages (the newest 240 kept). Think of Kīlauea's vog or a wildfire's smoke. A steady wind draws a thin plume; a turning wind bends it into an arc; in 3-D, a wind that changes with height (shear) tears it apart, because puffs at different heights go different ways. In 3-D the source stays at its height, like a chimney.
    Turbulent spread and mixing (on by default; untick to compare): real smoke doesn't follow one thread, it spreads as eddies stir it. So each puff also takes a random step every hour, as dispersion models' particles do, sized by an eddy diffusivity of 10,000 m²/s sideways: the plume widens with the square root of time, about 40 km across after a day (11 km without). In 3-D, a puff inside the boundary layer (GFS's own depth, the layer stirred by the surface) is mixed through it in an hour or so and held under its top, as vog is held under the trade inversion; above it, puffs barely mix (0.5 m²/s). ECMWF's files here have no boundary-layer depth, so with ECMWF the puffs only spread. One caution: drop a source on a mountain island, Kīlauea say, and the puffs may climb several km. In GFS's half-degree terrain the summit is a smooth 1,300 m dome the trade wind blows up and over (6 cm/s of rising air in one test); in reality the trades mostly split around Mauna Loa and Mauna Kea, which is why the vog drifts round to Kona. Over the open sea the same source stays in the lowest 300–800 m for the first day.
  • Weather balloon (lift's fly it as a weather balloon option; its own button until 2026-10-09, folded into lift since the probe, lift and the balloon all showed the same sounding). Tick it and click a spot to launch a radiosonde at the time on screen, the way the Weather Service does at 00 and 12 UTC from Līhuʻe and Hilo (and about 900 places worldwide). It rises about 5 m/s, 300 m a minute, and drifts with the model's wind at its height and time (the red line, at true height). Where it crosses each pressure level it reads that level's temperature, humidity and wind at its own position: a sounding. The Drop tab draws it on the same chart as lift, with the surface parcel lifted through it, which is how forecasters read a sounding (cloud base, how high a cloud could grow, the lid), and lists the wind at each level: watch it turn and strengthen with height. The model's data stop at 250 mb (about 10.5–11 km), so the flight ends there after about 35 minutes; a real balloon carries on to about 30 km, swelling to the size of a house before it bursts, and the instrument comes down on a parachute. In one test from Līhuʻe it reached 11 km in 36 minutes, 22 km to the north, the wind turning from the east-south-east at the surface to the south-south-west at 300 mb.
  • Forces on the air. Click a spot and arrows show what pushes the air there, on the focus level, at the nearer real step. Red, the pressure push: from high toward low pressure (at the surface, from sea-level pressure) or from high toward low heights (aloft), stronger where the lines are closer. Blue, Earth's spin (the Coriolis effect): at right angles to the wind, to its right in the northern hemisphere (left in the south), stronger for faster wind and nearer the poles, zero at the equator. Grey, what's left: if the air isn't speeding up, slowing or turning much, the three add to nothing, so at the surface this is friction, the ground dragging the wind back; aloft, where friction is small, it's mostly the wind curving round a trough or ridge, or changing speed. White is the wind, and yellow the geostrophic wind: the wind at which push and spin balance exactly, running along the lines. The forces are in knots of wind gained per hour (a push of 7 kt per hour, unbalanced, would add 7 kt to the wind in an hour). The arrows are sized to the screen, forces and winds each to their own scale; the labels give the values, and the Drop tab says it in words. What to see: aloft, push and spin nearly cancel and the wind runs along the height lines at close to the geostrophic speed (in one test at 500 mb, 42°N: push 10.7, spin 12.8 kt per hour, wind 36 kt within 5° of the lines, geostrophic 30); at the surface, friction holds the wind below the geostrophic and turns it across the isobars toward the low (in the trades north of Hawaiʻi: 20 kt against 37, crossing at 22°). That turning is why surface air spirals into lows (and rises) and out of highs (and sinks). Where the wind is faster than the geostrophic (often round a high) it isn't in balance, and the text says so. Within 5° of the equator there's no geostrophic wind: the spin is too weak to balance the push.
  • Lift a parcel. Click a spot and a parcel of its surface air is lifted through the model's column at the time on screen (it follows the column as you play). It starts at the 2 m temperature with the moisture of the lowest level above the ground (the files have no 2 m humidity; near the surface the air is well mixed). Rising, it expands and cools at the dry rate, 9.8 °C per km, until it is saturated: its cloud base (the lifting condensation level, roughly 125 m for every degree between the temperature and the dew point). Above that it cools at the slower moist rate (about 4–7 °C per km), because its condensing vapour gives up heat. Wherever it is warmer than the air around it, it is buoyant and rises by itself; where it is colder, it sinks back. The chart in the Drop tab is a simple sounding (temperature across, height up): the air around in red, its dew point in green, the parcel dashed white, shaded orange where it is buoyant and blue where it would have to be pushed. The label and text give the cloud base, the top it reaches by itself (the equilibrium level) and the CAPE, the energy of its buoyancy (under 100 J/kg: very little; over 1,000: storms). Warm the surface air adds 2–6 °C, as the afternoon sun does over land: a capped cloud can become a shower. In one build's columns: trade air west of Hawaiʻi had its cloud base at 630 m and could rise to 9.9 km (CAPE 302 J/kg); the ITCZ, 800 m and 11 km (512); air under a North Pacific storm's stable layer stopped at 1.6 km.
    The lid: the lowest layer above the ground where the air warms with height (an inversion), or failing that the most stable within 5 km (less than 3 °C per km), shaded violet. The trade-wind inversion, typically at 1.5–2.5 km, is why trade cumulus are flat-topped and why the wind is squeezed through the channels under it (the Alenuihāhā case). The model has only 7 levels, so a sharp inversion falls between two of them (850 mb, about 1.5 km, and 700 mb, about 3 km) and shows as a very stable layer there, not as warming: read the shading, not the exact heights. The parcel also ignores mixing with the air around it, which in real clouds keeps the tops lower.
  • Ring of balloons. 24 balloons set in a circle (radius 150–1000 km), joined by a yellow line. Press Play and watch its shape: near a front it is stretched long and thin (deformation, the process that sharpens fronts); drawn into a low its area shrinks, because the air is converging (and, having nowhere else to go, rising); under a high it spreads out, because the air is diverging as it sinks. The label gives its area as a percentage of the start. As the ring stretches, a new balloon is put in wherever two neighbours drift more than about 1.5 times their starting spacing apart, so the yellow line follows the filament instead of cutting across it (the 24 original balloons are the dots). On a level, you'll usually see the area change far less than the shape: the large-scale wind is close to non-divergent, so the ring is mostly stretched and folded, not squeezed. In a test on the 500 mb level, a 300 km ring at 30°N 160°W had an outline 12 times longer after three days, and an area of 98 % of the start. If part of the ring leaves the map, it's no longer closed, and the label says so instead of giving an area. On a level, area change is the convergence (or divergence) itself; in 3-D, the balloons also move to different heights, so read the shape more than the number.

Recipes: how to find things

Short procedures for questions people actually ask. Each says what you should find, and what can fool you.

Find the jet stream and read its speed

  1. Explore → "The jet stream" (250 mb coloured by wind speed, with particles).
  2. Hover along the red-to-purple ribbon. In winter the core off Japan often passes 150 kt; in summer it's weaker and further north. The fastest stretches are jet streaks.
  3. For its depth and shape, turn on the Slice across it (north–south through 150°E, say). The flat chart shows the core as a bullseye; hover gives its height and speed.
  4. For its 3-D form: 3-D tab, a layer from 6 to 12.5 km, quantity wind speed, Colour fill as a shape at 100 kt: the core is a tube. Add 3-D flow lines coloured by speed to see the air race through it.

Watch out: 300 mb can carry the core in summer or at low latitudes. If 250 mb looks weak, check 300.

Follow one storm through its life

  1. "Lows, highs and the jet above them". Find a young low (often near Japan or east of it, on the jet's poleward side).
  2. Step forward 6 or 12 hours at a time (→ steps between real times). Its label gives the central pressure.
  3. Is it a "bomb"? Meteorologists call a storm explosive if its central pressure falls at least 24 hPa in 24 h at 60°N, scaled by latitude (× sin(latitude) / sin(60°): about 20 hPa at 45°N, 16 hPa at 35°N). The North Pacific produces many in winter, along the warm Kuroshio current.
  4. Switch on 500 mb lines: while the storm deepens, the 500 mb trough sits west of the surface low (the storm "leans back"). When the two line up vertically, it stops deepening and starts to fill.
  5. "A storm in 3-D" flies to the deepest low with every level stacked above it, to see the lean directly.

Watch out: the deepest low in the data may be a typhoon or hurricane, not your storm. And the 0.5° grid makes small intense storms too shallow (see typhoons and hurricanes).

How confident is the forecast?

  1. Pin a probe at a place you care about (click it). In the meteogram, tick both models.
  2. Where the two pressure lines stay together, both models tell the same story: more confidence. Where they part (often after day 3–5), the forecast is uncertain; neither line is "the" answer.
  3. Press M while playing to flip the whole map between GFS and ECMWF at the same time.
  4. Or see the disagreement directly: colour the surface by sea-level pressure, tick as GFS − ECMWF, and play. Red and blue grow out of white as the week goes on; pairs of red and blue mark lows the two models put in different places (see GFS − ECMWF).

Two models agreeing isn't proof: they share observations and can share errors. Disagreeing is a strong sign of real uncertainty.

Estimate the wind from the contours

Away from the equator the wind aloft blows along the contours at a speed set by how close they are (the geostrophic wind; see contours and wind). A rule of thumb at 500 mb, around 40°N: 60 m contours 300 km apart mean about 40 kt. Half the spacing, double the wind. Measure spacing north–south, where 1° of latitude is 111 km. At the surface, around 45°N, 4 hPa isobars 300 km apart give about 20 kt of geostrophic wind; the real 10 m wind over the sea is a bit weaker and turned 10–20° toward the low.

Watch out: on the flat (Mercator) map, distances at 60°N are drawn twice as long as at the equator, so contours there look further apart than they are. Count degrees of latitude, or use the globe.

Find the rain–snow line (with thickness)

  1. Pin a probe. Its table gives the height of each level.
  2. Thickness = height of 500 mb − height of 1000 mb. It's proportional to the layer's average temperature: about 20 m for each 1 °C.
  3. Forecasters' rule of thumb: near 5,400 m (540 decametres), precipitation can fall as either rain or snow; well below, snow; well above, rain. Over the ocean, the sea's warmth usually keeps the lowest air too warm for snow even when thickness is low.

Read a cross-section through a front

  1. "Warm and cold air: fronts" shows 850 mb temperature. Find where the colours crowd together.
  2. Slice across that zone (north–south). In the flat chart the θ lines (potential temperature) crowd into a band that slopes up toward the cold side: the front. The jet core usually sits above its top.
  3. Colour the slice by humidity: moist air rides up the sloping front on the warm side; that's where the frontal cloud and rain are.

Find where clear-air turbulence is likely

  1. 3-D tab: a layer from 7 to 12.5 km (the cruising heights of airliners, about FL230–FL410), quantity turbulence risk, Colour fill as a shape at 8 (moderate).
  2. Add the jet for context: Layers, focus 250 mb, colour by wind speed, fill opacity 0.3.
  3. The fill appears as thin sheets above and below the jet core, where the wind changes fastest with height, and where the jet curves round sharp troughs and ridges. Hover the 250 mb level there to read the jet's speed.
  4. Switch to a wall across the jet with Plane on: the flat chart shows the risk as bands hugging the jet core's top and bottom edges.

Watch out: it's a risk index from the model's 0.5° grid and seven levels, not a report of bumps. Real turbulence is patchy on scales of a few km; aviation forecasts (SIGMETs, the Graphical Turbulence Guidance) use many more indices and pilots' reports.

See a front in 3-D

  1. Find a front: Layers, focus 850 mb, colour by thermal gradient. The front is a band above about 1.2 K per 100 km, usually trailing south-west from a low.
  2. 3-D tab: a box around that band, from the surface to 8 km, quantity thermal gradient, Colour fill as a shape at 1.2. Press Edge-on or Orbit it: the front is a sheet that leans back over the cold air with height, as the textbooks draw it.
  3. Switch the quantity to θe, fill everywhere: warm, moist air (orange) on one side, cold, dry air (blue) on the other, meeting along the sheet you just saw.
  4. Switch to frontogenesis: red where the wind is squeezing the front sharper (it's strengthening), blue where it's being pulled apart (weakening).
  5. Compare with the forecasters: pick OPC surface by the timeline and see where their drawn front falls.

Find where a storm will deepen

  1. A box round a young surface low and the trough to its west, surface to 10 km.
  2. Quantity divergence, fill as a shape at 4. Look for blue low down (air converging into the low) under red high up (air spreading out near the jet): air is being pulled up through the column between them, and the surface pressure falls. A storm with that pattern stacked over it is deepening.
  3. Switch to vorticity at 8: a red blob aloft moving east toward the low (the trough's spin) is the kick that often starts rapid deepening.
  4. Step forward 12 hours (→) and read the low's central pressure on its L label.

Find the tropopause

  1. A wall north–south along 180°, the whole atmosphere, Plane on, quantity stability.
  2. The flat chart shows a jump from 3–5 K per km (the troposphere, where weather happens) to well over 10 (the stratosphere) at the tropopause: low over the Arctic (8–10 km), high over the tropics (16 km, above the top of this data), stepping down sharply under the jet.
  3. Under a deep trough the step dips lower: stratospheric air folding down, which brings dry, ozone-rich air toward the storm.

Does the model see the clouds?

  1. "The model against the satellite": 500 mb coloured by moist air only (RH ≥ 70 %, drier air see-through) over the infrared image.
  2. Press B to blink the model on and off. Cloud bands and moist bands should line up. The live note scores it: the share of cold cloud tops the model has moist, and the share of moist model air under cold cloud.
  3. Move the time within the satellite day; the score changes.

Moist air isn't cloud, and 500 mb is one level: thin cirrus above or low cloud below won't match.

How unusual is this week?

  1. "How unusual is it?" (500 mb height anomaly). Press Play.
  2. Blobs that drift east are the normal parade of troughs and ridges. A big red blob that stays put for days, with blue either side or below it, is a block: storms go around it (see waves and blocks).
  3. For the ocean: "Warm and cold water" (observed). A broad warm patch is a marine heatwave; the equatorial band tells El Niño from La Niña (see the ocean underneath).

Check the model against the forecasters

  1. Pick a chart by the timeline (OPC surface, OPC 500 mb). Orange ticks show when each is valid; ◀ ▶ jump to one.
  2. The chart lies under the model's lines, which turn magenta. Compare the lows' positions and central pressures.
  3. Fronts are drawn only by the forecasters. Find where the model's isobars kink and its winds turn sharply: that's where a front is in the model.

Make a picture or animation for class

  1. Set up a view. On the Share tab press + Stop; add a few more views; Play flies between them.
  2. Save the view: a PNG of now, an MP4 or GIF over a time range (every real step, or hourly with the in-between frames marked "interpolated"), or of the tour. The colour keys, time, labels and credits are included; the controls are not.
  3. For projectors: turn the vertical stretch down a little, pick fewer levels, and use the light particle colours over the satellite.

Part II — The weather behind it

Pressure levels are heights

Pressure falls with height, so a pressure level is a surface in the air. The 500 mb surface is where half the atmosphere's mass is below you. Its height varies from place to place and day to day, and those height differences are the weather map aloft. The explorer draws each level at its real height, stretched vertically because the atmosphere is thin: about 10 km of weather over a 12,000 km map.

Numbers to remember. Near sea level, 1 hPa of pressure is about 8 m of height. 500 mb is about 5.5 km up, 250 mb about 10.4 km; above 250 mb is roughly where airliners cruise. Half the atmosphere's mass is below 500 mb, about 90 % below 250 mb.

Read it here. The level table gives each level's mean height over the data right now; the ruler stands at the view's centre and shows the levels' real heights there. Compare the ruler in the tropics and over the Bering Sea: every level is lower over the cold north. Common misreading: the sheets on screen look steep because they're stretched (×120 by default, plus relief if it's on). A real pressure level rises only a few hundred metres over thousands of km.

Contours and wind

On a pressure level the lines are height contours. At the surface they're isobars (lines of equal sea-level pressure, every 4 hPa). Away from the equator and above the friction of the surface, the wind blows almost along the contours. The pull from high to low heights is balanced by the Coriolis effect of the Earth's rotation. This is the geostrophic wind:

  • In the northern hemisphere, low heights are on your left with the wind at your back; in the southern hemisphere, on your right.
  • Tighter contours mean faster wind.
  • At the surface, friction slows the wind, and it turns partly across the isobars toward low pressure.
Near the equator this breaks down. The Coriolis effect is proportional to the sine of latitude and vanishes at the equator. Within about 10–15° of it, winds no longer follow the contours, which are flat there. Follow the particles and barbs instead.

Three ways to draw the wind

The same wind field, three pictures, each good at something different. Tick any mix on the focus level:

  • Particles move. Your eye picks up speed at once (fast streaks in the jet, crawling ones in a high) and the animation shows air converging into a low or spreading out of a high. They're a feel for the flow, not measurements: no single streak can be read.
  • Barbs are readings: a speed in knots and a direction at a set of points, the way forecasters and the OPC charts give wind. Best for "what is the wind here?", poor for the pattern between the points.
  • Flow lines (streamlines) are lines drawn everywhere parallel to the wind at one moment. They show the pattern whole: where the air turns around a low, where lines spread apart (diffluence, air diverging: often rising air and weather downstream of a trough aloft) or run together (confluence), and near the equator, where contours say little, where the trade winds meet. They say nothing about speed: spacing is set by the drawing, not the wind. Pair them with the wind-speed fill or barbs.
Try it. Focus 250 mb, colour by wind, tick flow lines, and leave Surface particles show: 10 m wind on: the jet's lines above, the surface wind moving below. Where the lines aloft fan apart ahead of a trough, look under it for a deepening surface low.
Streamlines are not trajectories. A flow line is a snapshot. As the pattern moves, real air parcels leave it: a parcel starting on a line ahead of a moving trough doesn't stay on that line. Only when the pattern stands still do the two agree.

How fast? The geostrophic wind speed is V = (g / f) × (height change / distance), where f = 2Ω sin(latitude) is the Coriolis parameter (Ω = the Earth's rotation rate). The same contour spacing means faster wind nearer the equator, because f is smaller there: at 20°N almost twice as fast as at 40°N. A worked example is in the recipes.

Read it here. Colour a level by wind speed and show its height contours: the fastest colours sit where the contours crowd. Turn barbs on to see the wind cross the surface isobars at a small angle toward low pressure, while aloft it runs along the contours.

Highs, lows and clouds

A low (cyclone) has air spiralling in and rising, so clouds and rain. In the northern hemisphere it turns counter-clockwise. A high (anticyclone) has air sinking and spreading out, so usually fair skies; it turns clockwise in the northern hemisphere. Over the North Pacific, the big features are the subtropical North Pacific High and, especially in the cold season, a procession of lows along the storm track that often deepen near the Aleutians. The deepest low in the data is not always a mid-latitude storm: a tropical cyclone (typhoon, hurricane) can be deeper and far smaller.

Try: "Where's the weather now?". Isobars over the newest satellite image. Match the cloud swirls to the lows, then compare with OPC's own Pacific surface analysis.
Read it here. In the infrared, a mature mid-latitude storm's cloud often looks like a comma: a long tail (the cold front's cloud band) curling into a head around the low. The low's centre is usually under the dry slot or the head's hook, not under the brightest cloud. Common misreading: the highest pressure is not always where the weather is best: sinking air under a high can trap low cloud and fog over cold water.

The jet stream

A river of fast wind near the top of the weather, around 250–300 mb. It forms where cold polar air meets warm subtropical air: the temperature contrast makes the wind increase with height (the thermal wind). Off Japan the Pacific jet is among the strongest on Earth, especially in winter, when its core can exceed 180 kt. Its fastest parts are jet streaks. Air spreading out aloft near a streak's exit lets surface pressure fall beneath it, which is how the jet feeds storms.

Try: "The jet stream" (250 mb wind speed with particles), then play the timeline: jet streaks run along it and the pattern shifts east.

Where the jet makes storms. Air accelerating into a jet streak and slowing out of it spreads apart aloft in two of the streak's four quarters: on the northern hemisphere's right entrance (equatorward side, upstream end) and left exit (poleward side, downstream end). Air spreading aloft must be replaced from below, so surface pressure falls and air rises there: favoured spots for storms to form and deepen.

Read it here. Find a streak (the fastest colours at 250 mb). Show the surface isobars as context and play: do lows deepen under its poleward-downstream end? Slice across the jet: the flat chart shows the core as a bullseye and the tropopause (θ lines packed tight above it) dropping on the jet's cold side. In 3-D: a layer from 6 to 12.5 km with the colour fill at 100 kt shows the jet as a tube; switch the quantity to divergence and the red (spreading) blobs sit at the streaks' left exits and right entrances, as the four-quarter rule says. Turbulence risk shows where the jet's shear is strongest.

Troughs, ridges and storms

At 500 mb the height contours wave north and south. Ridges bulge poleward (warm air, fair weather beneath) and troughs dip equatorward (cold air aloft). Surface lows tend to form and deepen east of troughs, where the air aloft diverges. They drift with the 500 mb flow at roughly half its speed.

A growing storm leans west with height: the 500 mb low sits upstream of the surface low. As the storm matures, the centres line up vertically and it stops deepening.

In 3-D: a box round the low, vorticity as a shape: a red column that leans west with height while the storm deepens and stands upright once it's mature (see the walk-through).

Waves in the westerlies, and blocks

Seen from above the pole, the mid-latitude westerlies don't blow in a circle: they meander in a few great waves, the Rossby waves, named after the meteorologist who explained them. Their troughs carry cold air toward the equator and their ridges carry warm air poleward, and they exist because the Coriolis effect grows with latitude: air pushed north is turned back south, and vice versa.

  • Long waves: four to seven around the hemisphere, thousands of km across. They move slowly, sometimes not at all, and set the pattern for a week or more.
  • Short waves: smaller troughs riding through the long waves, typically 10–15° of longitude a day. Each one usually has a surface low developing ahead (east) of it: these are the "storm makers".
  • Blocks: when a ridge amplifies until it pinches off, or a high sits poleward of a low, the flow splits around it and the pattern locks for five days or more. Storms detour around the block; weather under and beside it (heat, drought, a stalled cold pool, repeated rain) persists. North Pacific blocks are common near the Aleutians and the Gulf of Alaska, especially in late winter and spring.

Fronts

Fronts are the boundaries between air masses, where temperature changes quickly. They show best at 850 mb, above the sea surface's smoothing influence, where the temperature colours crowd together.

  • Cold front: colder air pushes in behind. In the northern hemisphere the wind veers across it, from south-westerly to westerly or north-westerly.
  • Warm front: warm air rides up over cold air ahead of a low.

Fronts slope: the cold air is a shallow wedge, so a front aloft sits behind its surface position.

Read it here. "Warm and cold air: fronts": 850 mb temperature with barbs. A front is where the colours crowd into a narrow band; along a cold front the barbs turn sharply (veer) as you cross it, and the surface isobars kink into a "V" pointing away from the low (a trough). Slice across it to see the slope. Common misreading: the model never draws a front; fronts on OPC's charts are a forecaster's analysis of exactly these clues.

Three fields that find fronts. Forecasters locate fronts with the thermal gradient (a front is a band of strong gradient: the NWS standard is about 6 °C over 500 km, 1.2 K per 100 km here), with θe (which includes moisture, so it separates warm humid air from cold dry air even where temperatures are close), and with frontogenesis (is the wind making the gradient sharper or weaker?). Thickness: a layer's temperature

The height difference between two pressure levels is the layer's thickness. Warm air is less dense, so the same mass of air (the same pressure drop) fills a thicker layer. The relation is exact (the hypsometric equation): thickness = (R × mean temperature / g) × ln(p₁ / p₂). For 1000–500 mb that's about 20 m for each kelvin of the layer's mean temperature: 5,400 m at −7 °C mean, 5,700 m at +8 °C.

  • You can see thickness in 3-D. In the Oblique view, show two levels (say 1000 and 500 mb) as sheets: the gap between them is the thickness, stretched. It's wider over the tropics and narrower over the Bering Sea. That gap is the layer's temperature.
  • Thickness and wind shear. Where thickness changes quickly across the map (a strong temperature contrast), the wind must increase with height: the thermal wind. It blows along the thickness lines with cold air on the left (northern hemisphere). That's why the jet stream sits above the sharpest temperature contrast.
  • Warm and cold advection. If the wind blows from warm toward cold air, it turns clockwise with height ("veers"); from cold toward warm, it turns anticlockwise ("backs"). The probe's wind column shows it.
  • The 540 line. Forecasters use 5,400 m (540 decametres) of 1000–500 mb thickness as a rough rain/snow boundary over land.

Trade winds and the ITCZ

South of the North Pacific High, air flows back toward the equator and is turned west by the Earth's rotation: the north-east trade winds. They're steady, 10–20 kt, and bring Hawaiʻi its weather. The southern hemisphere has south-east trades. Where the two meet, near 5–10°N over most of the Pacific, is the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ): rising air, tall thunderstorms, and light winds beneath. Sailors called them the doldrums. In the South Pacific, the SPCZ slants south-east from near the Solomon Islands.

Typhoons and hurricanes

Tropical cyclones are the same kind of storm under different names: typhoon west of the dateline, hurricane east of it (the Central Pacific Hurricane Center in Honolulu watches 140°W–180°). A tropical storm has sustained winds of at least 34 kt, a typhoon or hurricane at least 64 kt. They draw their energy from warm ocean water, usually at least 26.5 °C, through evaporation and the heat released as the vapour condenses in their thunderstorms.

  • Warm core. Unlike a mid-latitude storm, a tropical cyclone has no temperature front, doesn't lean with height, and is warmest at its centre, aloft. Its winds are strongest near the surface and weaken upward, and above it sits a high with air spiralling out.
  • Tracks. Steered by the subtropical high: they move west on its southern side, curve north around its western end and, reaching the westerlies, recurve north-east. Many then become powerful extratropical storms over the North Pacific in autumn (extratropical transition).
  • Small and fierce. The eye is 20–50 km across, smaller than one grid square here (0.5°, ≈55 km). So on this grid a strong cyclone is too broad and too shallow. On 28 Sep 2026 the Honolulu forecasters analysed Hurricane Nolo at 929 hPa; on the explorer's 0.5° grid the model's lowest point there was about 974 hPa. The storm is there, but its intensity isn't.

Atmospheric rivers

Long (thousands of km), narrow corridors of moist air, usually ahead of a cold front, carrying water vapour from the tropics or subtropics toward the mid-latitudes. Landfalling on the US West Coast or BC, one can bring days of heavy rain. The "Pineapple Express" runs from near Hawaiʻi. Forecasters map them by precipitable water (the column's total water vapour, in mm) or by vapour transport. The explorer has precipitable water as the surface's water vapour fill (both models: GFS "PWAT", ECMWF "tcwv"), for the forecast week and, in the Past year, for every day of the last year.

How to recognise one. Common working definitions: a filament of high water vapour at least 2,000 km long and less than about 1,000 km wide, with vapour transport of at least 250 kg per metre per second. Most of the water moves in the lowest 3 km, which is why 700 and 850 mb humidity with strong wind shows them.

The big circulation

Averaged over time, air rises at the ITCZ, flows poleward aloft, and sinks around 30°N and 30°S, building the subtropical highs with their clear skies. It returns toward the equator as the trades. This is the Hadley cell. Poleward of it, the mid-latitudes are dominated by the jet and its travelling storms, which carry heat poleward. With the data reaching 20°S, both halves of the tropics are in view.

Rising and sinking air

Almost all the wind is horizontal: tens of metres per second sideways, but only centimetres per second up or down. That tiny vertical part makes the weather. Rising air cools as it expands, its water vapour condenses, and cloud and rain form; sinking air warms and dries, so skies clear. The models compute it, and the explorer shows it.

  • Where it comes from. Both models publish the pressure vertical velocity ω ("omega"), in pascals per second: how fast a parcel's pressure changes. Rising air moves to lower pressure, so ω is negative for rising air. The build turns it into an ordinary speed, w in cm/s with + meaning up, using the hydrostatic relation w = −ω R T / (p g) (R = 287 J/kg/K, g = 9.81 m/s²). At 500 mb and −20 °C, ω = −1 Pa/s is about 15 cm/s up.
  • How big. On this 0.5° grid (about 50 km), most of the air moves up or down at 1–5 cm/s. Ahead of a strong storm, along its warm front and in its "warm conveyor belt" (the stream of warm, moist air that climbs over the front), 10–30 cm/s, sometimes more: that's 400–1,000 m an hour, enough to lift air from the sea to the jet in half a day. Thunderstorm updrafts reach 10–30 m/s, but they're only a few km wide, far smaller than a grid box; the model represents them by their average effect, so they show only as broad, weaker rising.
  • Where to look. Rising: east of (ahead of) upper troughs, where the flow aloft spreads apart; along fronts; in the ITCZ; in typhoons. Sinking: behind cold fronts, under ridges and highs, and over the subtropics (the downward half of the Hadley cell; see the big circulation).

Five ways to see it. (1) Colour a level by rising / sinking air (Layers, "Colour by"): 500 or 700 mb show it best. Weak motion (under 0.5 cm/s) is left see-through. (2) The slice's vertical motion colour, and motion arrows on its flat chart. (3) The 3-D particles' rise and sink. (4) The colour fill of rising / sinking air in the 3-D tab. (5) 3-D flow lines with rise and sink, which draw the climbing streams as whole ribbons. And to see why it rises, look at divergence: air converging low down and spreading aloft has to go up in between (see spin, spreading, turbulence and stability). Hovering a level reads it out: "rising 12 cm/s (430 m an hour)".

Spin, spreading, turbulence and stability

Temperature, wind and humidity say what the air is. The derived quantities say what it's doing: spinning, spreading, shearing, resisting being lifted. Meteorologists read the weather's development from them. The explorer works them out from each level's fields (see what they mean for units and ranges); this section explains why they matter.

Vorticity: the air's spin

The Earth's rotation gives every column of air a background spin, the Coriolis parameter f = 2Ω sin(latitude): about 10 ×10⁻⁵ per second at 45°N, zero at the equator. On top of that the air has its own spin relative to the ground, the relative vorticity the explorer shows, which in storms and troughs reaches ±10–20: as big as the Earth's own. The two together, the absolute vorticity, behave like a spinning skater's: a column of air keeps its spin divided by its depth (its potential vorticity) unless heating or friction change it. Stretch a column and it spins faster; squash it and it slows.

  • That's why storms spin up: air converging into a low is stretched upward, and its spin concentrates.
  • Troughs aloft carry high vorticity; as the westerlies blow it east out of a trough toward a ridge, the air must lose spin, which it does by spreading out aloft. Spreading aloft pulls air up from below. So air rises, and surface lows form, just downstream (east) of an upper trough: the forecaster's rule "positive vorticity advection means rising motion".
  • Along the jet, the wind drops off either side of the core, so there's a stripe of cyclonic spin on its cold (poleward) side and anticyclonic on its warm side, even where the flow is straight.

Divergence: spreading and converging

Air that spreads out horizontally (divergence) must be replaced, and air that piles in (convergence) must go somewhere: in the atmosphere, up or down. Over a developing low, air converges near the surface and diverges near the jet; somewhere in between, around 500–600 mb, the two cancel (the level of non-divergence). The column between rises. Over a high it's the reverse: convergence aloft, divergence at the surface, sinking between. The surface pressure changes by the small difference between the two: if the spreading aloft outpaces the inflow below, the column loses mass and the low deepens.

In the explorer: a box through a storm with divergence as a shape (±4): blue low down, red high up over the rising air, the reverse behind the cold front. Switch to rising/sinking air and the rising column sits between them. Values of ±4 ×10⁻⁵ per second are about 14 % of the air in a column spreading out every hour.

Turbulence: when the wind shears

Where the wind changes fast with height, the layers slide over each other. If the shear is strong compared with the air's stability, the interface curls into breaking waves (Kelvin–Helmholtz waves, sometimes visible as rows of curling clouds) and the air becomes turbulent. With no cloud to warn of it, pilots call it clear-air turbulence. Ellrod and Knapp (1992) found that the product of the vertical wind shear and the horizontal deformation (how much the flow stretches and squeezes the air, which sharpens the shear) marks where it's likely. That product is the turbulence index here. It's highest above and below the jet core, where it bends round sharp troughs and ridges, and where two jets merge.

Stability: the lid on the weather

Lift a parcel of dry air and it cools at 9.8 °C per km; its potential temperature θ stays the same. If the air around it has higher θ above, the lifted parcel is colder than its new surroundings and sinks back: the air is stable, and dθ/dz, the stability here, is positive. The ordinary troposphere has 3–5 K per km: stable enough that air needs a push (a front, a mountain, convergence) to rise, but it can. At the tropopause the temperature stops falling with height, and dθ/dz jumps above 10: the stratosphere is a lid, which is why thunderstorm tops spread out flat into anvils. Low down, an inversion (temperature rising with height) is a lid too: the trade-wind inversion about 1.5–2.5 km up over the subtropical Pacific caps the trade cumulus, and the shallow marine layer off California keeps its stratus in. Both show as high stability in a thin layer.

θe: air masses and instability

Equivalent potential temperature counts the heat the air would gain if all its water vapour condensed, so it stays the same when air rises and forms cloud. That makes it the best tracer of air masses: tropical air over warm water has θe near 350 K at the surface, polar air in winter under 280 K, and the boundary between them is sharp. Where θe falls with height, the air is potentially unstable: lift the whole layer until it saturates, and the lower part becomes warmer than the upper and it overturns into showers and thunderstorms. Where it rises with height, lifting only makes steady cloud and rain.

Vertical mixing: the boundary layer and the Richardson number

Air near the ground is stirred by the ground itself: heated from below on a sunny afternoon, it rises in thermals; dragged by friction under a strong wind, it tumbles into eddies. The layer this stirring reaches is the boundary layer (or mixed layer). Everything released at the surface (smoke, haze, pollen, moisture, heat) spreads through it and, mostly, stays in it, under a lid of stable air on top. And it works the other way: fast wind just above the ground reaches the surface as gusts only as deep as the mixing goes. Two readouts show it.

Boundary-layer depth (GFS)

Layers, surface, colour fill "boundary-layer depth": GFS's own diagnosis of how deep the mixing reaches (its HPBL field, in metres), pale for a few hundred metres, dark red for 2–3 km. ECMWF's open data doesn't include it, so with ECMWF on screen nothing is coloured. The probe adds "mixed … deep" to the surface line. Typical values:

  • Over the open ocean: 500–1500 m, changing slowly, because the sea's temperature hardly changes through the day. It's shallow where warm air flows over cold water (the California current, where a marine layer 300–500 m deep keeps its stratus in), and deep where cold air pours over warmer sea behind a cold front, when the sea heats it from below and showers pop up.
  • Over land: it swells every afternoon (1–3 km, more over deserts in summer) and collapses at night to a few hundred metres or less, when the ground cools and a surface inversion forms. Press Play and the land pulses once a day. Smoke from a fire spreads high in the afternoon and settles into valleys at night.
  • Under a big high: sinking air warms and caps the boundary layer low, which is why stagnant highs bring haze, fog and poor air.

The Richardson number: stability against shear

Whether a layer mixes is a contest. Its stability (θ increasing with height, see stability) resists being overturned; the wind shear across it (the wind changing with height) tries to overturn it. The Richardson number is the ratio: Ri = (g/θ) (Δθ/Δz) ÷ (ΔV/Δz)², stability divided by shear squared. Lewis Fry Richardson worked it out in 1920. The rule of thumb:

  • Ri below about 0.25 (yellow, orange): shear wins. The layer curls into Kelvin–Helmholtz waves and breaks into turbulence, mixing it top to bottom.
  • 0.25 to 1 (green): marginal. Turbulence already going may carry on; smooth flow stays smooth.
  • Above 1 (blue): stable. The layers slide over each other without mixing: an inversion, a calm clear night, the marine layer's lid.
  • Below 0 (red): θ falls with height, so the air overturns by itself, with or without shear: convection.

It appears in two places:

  • Layers, a level, "mixing from the ground: bulk Richardson number": Ri between the ground (2 m temperature, surface pressure, 10 m wind) and that level, the depth between them from the pressures (the hypsometric equation). It asks "can the ground's air be stirred up to here?". Weather models find the top of the boundary layer roughly this way, going up until the bulk Richardson number from the ground passes a critical value near 0.25, so on GFS the yellow-to-green edge at 925 or 850 mb and the boundary-layer depth should roughly agree. They do: on the 2026-09-30 build, where GFS's depth was over 1.6 km, 99 % of points were mixed (Ri below 0.25) up to 925 mb (about 0.8 km) and 67 % up to 850 mb (about 1.5 km); where it was under 800 m, 3 % reached 925 mb and none 850 mb. The probe adds "Ri from the ground" to the level's line.
  • 3-D tab, quantity "mixing: Richardson number": Ri of the layer from each model level up to the next, at every height, for slices, planes and the colour fill. As a shape, the fill shows the layers at or below the threshold, default 1, not 0.25. The model's levels are 1.5–4 km apart, and turbulence lives in layers tens to hundreds of metres thick; across a thick layer the shear is averaged down and Ri reads high. On the 2026-09-30 build, hardly any layer between levels reached 0.25, but about 8 % of the 300–250 mb layer, round the jet, was at or below 1. So at this resolution a higher cut, around 1, finds the likeliest places, which is also what forecasters do with coarse data. Aloft, compare it with the Ellrod index; near the ground, with the boundary-layer depth.

Channel and gap winds: the big pattern and the local squeeze

Wind blows because pressure differs from place to place, and away from the equator it blows along the isobars, faster where they're closer together (the geostrophic wind; near the ground friction slows it and turns it toward low pressure). Where mountains box the air into a channel or a gap, the wind can only blow along it, and it speeds up where the gap narrows. So the wind in a channel has two causes: the large-scale pattern (where the highs and lows are, and how the isobars run and curve) sets the push, and the terrain channels and accelerates it. A 0.5° grid (about 50 km) sees the first and not the second: it shows the push, and the channel or gap blows harder than the grid does.

  • The Salish Sea: the pairs NWS Seattle uses, in its sign convention (first minus second): Portland − Bellingham (positive: southerlies up Puget Sound; negative: northerlies), Quillayute − Bellingham (positive: westerlies down the Strait of Juan de Fuca; negative: easterlies out of it), Bellingham − Williams Lake, BC (strongly negative: the Fraser outflow).   • The Columbia Gorge: NWS Portland uses Troutdale − The Dalles (negative: east wind). Over the Cascades the note skips the isobars' push: sea-level pressure over mountains is an extrapolation.

What the numbers are. The isobars' push is the geostrophic wind from the pressure gradient at the place: in the tropics, where the Earth's spin is weak, it overstates the real wind a lot, so read it as "how hard the pattern pushes", comparable from day to day, not as a wind forecast.

The ocean underneath

The atmosphere and the ocean steer each other. The sea's surface temperature sets how much heat and moisture the air above can take up; the winds drive the currents and stir up cold water.

  • The warm pool and the cold tongue. The western tropical Pacific is the warmest large body of ocean water on Earth (often above 29 °C), feeding the tallest thunderstorms. The trade winds push surface water west and pull cold water up along the equator in the east: a cold tongue.
  • Currents. The Kuroshio carries warm water north past Japan, then east as the Kuroshio Extension; its sharp temperature edge is where winter storms deepen fastest. The California Current brings cold water south along North America, with upwelling near the coast, which is why San Francisco is foggy in summer.
  • El Niño and La Niña (ENSO). Every few years the trades weaken and the eastern equatorial Pacific warms (El Niño), or strengthen and it cools (La Niña). NOAA watches the Niño-3.4 region (5°S–5°N, 170°W–120°W), which lies inside this explorer's data: El Niño is declared at about +0.5 °C sustained. The shift moves the tropical thunderstorms, and with them the jet stream and the storm track around the world.
  • Marine heatwaves. Patches of unusually warm water lasting weeks to years. The best-known North Pacific one, "the Blob" of 2013–2016, disrupted fisheries and ecosystems along the whole West Coast.

Waves and swell

Wind blowing over the sea raises waves. How big they get depends on three things: the wind's speed, how long it blows (duration) and over how long a stretch of water (fetch). A North Pacific winter storm, with 50 kt winds over 1,000 km for a day or more, can raise seas of 10–15 m.

  • Significant wave height (Hs), the number on every marine forecast, is the average height of the highest third of the waves, trough to crest. Individual waves vary: about 1 in 10 is taller than 1.3 × Hs, and the largest in a few hours can reach about 2 × Hs.
  • Wind sea and swell. Under the wind, the waves are short, steep and jumbled: the wind sea. Once they leave the wind, they sort themselves by period and travel on as smooth, long swell, crossing whole oceans: winter swell from the Aleutians reaches Hawaiʻi's north shores two or three days later, and big Southern Ocean swell reaches Alaska.
  • Period and speed. The period T is the time between crests. Longer-period waves travel faster: in deep water a wave's crests move at g·T / 2π (about 1.56 × T m/s), and the energy (a group of waves) at half that, g·T / 4π (about 0.78 × T m/s). A 15-second swell's energy crosses the ocean at about 11.7 m/s, some 1,000 km a day. That's why the explorer's wave particles move at the group speed: they show where the energy is going and how fast. Surface particles show (Layers tab, "At the surface") picks the 10 m wind (blue, the default), wave energy (teal) or off, whatever level is in focus. Colour by wave height or wave period (long periods dark: old swell from far away).
  • Direction. Given, like wind, as where the waves come from. The particles move the other way, where the waves go.

The two wave models. Each atmosphere model drives its own wave model with its own winds, from the same run, so switching model (M) switches the sea state too:

  • GFS-Wave (NOAA's WAVEWATCH III), driven by GFS: significant height, the primary wave period and the primary direction (those of the biggest wave system).
  • ECMWF's wave model (ECWAM), coupled to ECMWF's atmosphere: significant height, the peak period and the mean direction (averaged over all the wave systems present).

Where one swell dominates, primary and mean directions agree; where two seas cross (a local wind sea over a distant swell), the mean direction falls between them. Measured on this build's data (24 h forecast from 28/12Z): the two models' heights correlate at 0.95, with GFS-Wave on average 0.14 m lower; where seas are above 2 m their directions differ by a median of 15°, largely that difference in definition.

Try: "Where are the big waves?" (height, with the wind as particles): the highest seas sit under and just behind each storm's strongest, longest-blowing winds. Then "Follow the swell" (period, with wave-energy particles): press Play and watch long-period swell stream away from the storms and fan out across the ocean. When does it reach Hawaiʻi? Hover for height, period and direction; a long period (14 s or more) means old, fast swell from far away. Watch out: the models' 0.5° grid (≈55 km) smooths the sea near coasts and islands, where waves are shaped by the sea bed and shelter. This is open-ocean guidance, not a surf or harbour forecast.

Satellite infrared and water vapour

From two geostationary satellites: GOES-18 (over 137.2°W) and Himawari-9 (over 140.7°E). The infrared channel (about 10.3 µm) measures the temperature of what it sees: cloud tops, or the surface where it's clear.

  • White means cold, and cold means high: deep storm clouds, the ITCZ, the jet's cirrus.
  • Low cloud and fog are nearly as warm as the sea, so they're hard to see in infrared.
  • Colour-enhanced colours tops colder than −30 °C in 10 °C bands.

How high is that cloud? In the lower atmosphere, temperature falls about 6.5 °C per km. A cloud top at −50 °C is roughly 9–11 km up (higher in the tropics, where the surface is warmer); at −70 °C or colder it's a thunderstorm top reaching the tropopause. Tops colder than the tropopause ("overshooting tops") mark the most violent storms.

Common misreading: the infrared shows temperature, not cloud thickness or rain. A thin veil of cirrus can look as bright as a raining thunderstorm; low cloud and fog, nearly as warm as the sea, look almost black.

Water vapour

The Map tab's Channel menu switches to water vapour (mid-level, about 6.9 µm: GOES-18 band 9, Himawari-9 band 9), at the same times as the infrared. Water vapour absorbs and re-emits at this wavelength, so the satellite can't see the surface: it sees the temperature of the highest moist layer, usually around 400–600 mb (5–7 km). Where the air aloft is moist, that layer is high and cold; where it's dry, the satellite sees deeper, warmer air. So it shows moisture aloft even where there's no cloud at all, and the whole flow pattern with it.

  • White and pale blue (colder than about −45 °C): high cloud, or very moist air aloft.
  • Blue: moist air aloft. Grey: in between.
  • Brown to orange (warmer than about −25 °C): dry air aloft, usually sinking: under the subtropical highs (the trade inversion's air), in the "dry slot" wrapping into a storm behind its front, and behind troughs.

What to look for: the swirl of an upper low or trough (often far clearer than in the infrared), the sharp edge between moist and dry air along a jet stream, dry intrusions curling into lows, and moisture plumes reaching from the tropics (an atmospheric river's upper part). Compare with the 500 mb heights: troughs and lows sit in the swirls, and the dry, sinking air lies behind them. Hover for the value. The colours are fixed (200 to 270 K) so they mean the same at every time.

Models, runs and time

A weather model starts from an analysis, its best estimate of the atmosphere now, blending millions of observations with its previous forecast. It then steps forward in time. Each start is a run, named by its start time in UTC (e.g. "28/00Z"). GFS (NOAA) and ECMWF's IFS each run four times a day.

  • Future times come from each model's newest complete run at build time.
  • Past times (the last 24 hours) come from earlier runs' analyses and 3-hour forecasts, the closest thing the models have to what happened. The label says which run.
  • Why models differ: different physics, resolution and use of observations. And the atmosphere is chaotic: small differences at the start grow. The models usually agree closely for a day or two and drift apart by days 5–7. Where they disagree, the forecast is uncertain.
Try: "GFS vs ECMWF", then press M while playing forward.

How good are the forecasts? A common measure is how well the 500 mb height pattern matches what happened. By that measure, today's best global models stay useful for about 8 to 9 days on average; ECMWF has usually led GFS by a few hours to a day. That's an average: some weeks are predictable for longer, and a single storm's exact track and depth can be wrong within 2–3 days.

Try: step forward to day 5–7 and flip M between the models: where do the lows differ? Pin a probe there with both models on. When the build is a day older, the "past" hours show how that forecast turned out.

Anomalies: how unusual is it?

A weather map shows what the air is doing; an anomaly shows how that compares with normal for the place, the date and the hour. It's the field minus its 1991–2020 average (the current World Meteorological Organization "normal" period). Red = above normal, blue = below, white = about normal. Fills offered when this build has the climatology (the Explore tab's fill menu):

  • Height anomaly and temperature anomaly on any pressure level. At 500 mb a big blue blob is a trough colder and lower than usual; a red one a ridge, and when it sits still for days, a block.
  • Pressure anomaly at sea level (hPa): storms stand out as deep blue, even in seasons when lows are common.
  • Sea temperature anomaly from the model's sea temperature, and, separately, observed sea temperature and its anomaly from NOAA's OISST: satellites, ships and buoys blended into one daily map, about a day behind. The observed map is one real day (its date is in the note above the map): it doesn't change as you move the time.

Where "normal" comes from. Two climatologies, both 1991–2020, downloaded once and kept (a few MB):

  • Air (heights, temperatures, sea-level pressure): the NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis, a 2.5° re-run of the past with one fixed model. It has averages for 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC, not just a daily mean. That matters: the atmosphere has a daily tide (the sun heats it twice a day), and over this domain the four hours differ by up to 2.6 hPa at sea level in the tropics and 20 m at 500 mb (measured). A single-hour model field minus a daily mean would paint that tide as "anomaly".
  • Sea: OISST's own 1991–2020 daily average at 0.25°. OISST's ready-made anomaly is against 1971–2000, so it isn't used: every anomaly here has the same baseline.

Smoothing. A 30-year average for one calendar day is still noisy: from one day to the next the raw averages jump by about 1 hPa (up to 8 hPa) and 11 m at 500 mb (measured), just from which storms happened to fall on that date. So, as NOAA's Climate Prediction Center does, each point's average is fitted with a smooth annual cycle: the mean plus four harmonics (periods of a year, a half, a third and a quarter).

Between the 6-hourly averages (03, 09, 15, 21 UTC steps) the average is interpolated in time. That misses part of the twice-daily tide: up to about 1 hPa at sea level in the tropics, less toward the poles, a few metres at 500 mb. The note above the map says so at those times.

Checked on real data (GFS, 28–30 Sep 2026): averaged over the whole domain, the anomalies came out at about +0.5 hPa, +5 to +14 m at 500 mb, +0.8 °C at 500 mb and +0.2 to +0.5 °C sea temperature: small, and warmer than the 1991–2020 normal, as expected for recent years. In the tropics the pressure anomaly stayed at 0.7–1.1 hPa through 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC. With a daily-mean normal it would have swung with the 2.6 hPa tide. The model sea-temperature anomaly is about 0.25 °C higher at 00 UTC than at 12 UTC: that is the sunlit sea skin warming in the afternoon near the dateline, against a daily-mean normal. It's real, but it isn't a change in the ocean.

An independent check of the baseline: averaged over the domain on 27 Sep 2026, the observed sea-temperature anomaly here (vs 1991–2020) was +0.93 °C, and OISST's own published anomaly (vs the older 1971–2000 normal) was +1.16 °C. The older normal is 0.23 °C cooler, about what two decades of ocean warming should give.

What an anomaly can't tell you. Part of any anomaly is the difference between today's model and the older, coarser reanalysis rather than the weather: small (a few metres, a few tenths of a degree) over the open ocean, larger near mountains. Treat the big patterns as real and the last few units as noise.

Try: "How unusual is it?", then press Play: do the blue and red blobs drift east with the flow, or stay put (a block)? And "Warm and cold water": find the warm or cold tongue along the equator, and any marine heatwave patch in the North Pacific.

Part III — Teaching with it

The past year

The forecast shows a week; the climatology shows a smooth average of thirty years. Neither shows what a year actually was: which storms came through, how long a warm spell lasted, when the sea warmed. The Past year mode keeps one real analysis for each of the last 365 days, the model's best estimate of the atmosphere at 00 UTC that day, made from the observations of the time.

  • Weather against climate. Play a year of 500 mb height anomaly: red and blue blobs (ridges and troughs stronger than normal) drift east day by day, and now and then one sits still for a week or more. That's a block, and the weather under it (heat, cold, drought or rain) sticks around with it.
  • The seasons. Switch to the actual 500 mb height or 850 mb temperature and play: the cold pool over the Bering Sea and Alaska swells in winter and shrinks in summer, and the jet (250 mb wind) moves north and weakens in summer, south and strengthens in winter.
  • The ocean's memory. Sea temperature anomaly changes slowly: a warm patch (a marine heatwave) can last for months while the atmosphere above it changes every few days. Click a point in it to see how long it lasted.
  • Storm tracks. Sea-level pressure anomaly, played fast, shows the winter storm track as a stream of blue lows from Japan to the Gulf of Alaska, and how quiet it gets in summer.
  • Steering. 500 mb height with its wind as flow lines: the lines run along the height contours (the geostrophic wind) and the storms at the surface ride along them. Step day by day and follow one trough across.
  • Atmospheric rivers. Water vapour with the 10 m wind: a long, narrow ribbon of blue (40 mm or more of water in the column, outside the tropics) reaching from near Hawaiʻi toward the West Coast is an atmospheric river, the "Pineapple Express". Click a point on the California coast: the chart's spikes are the year's rivers.
  • Sea ice. The Bering Sea ice edge grows south through the winter and melts back in spring. Click a point in the Bering Sea to see when it froze and when it cleared.
  • The year's biggest seas. Wave height, with the 10 m wind: the winter storms raise 8–12 m seas in the Gulf of Alaska and off Japan. Click a point near Hawaiʻi's north shore: the spikes in the chart are the big swell events, often days after a storm thousands of km away.
Read it here. Click a point in the Gulf of Alaska with observed sea temperature anomaly shown: the chart gives the whole year there; a line that stays above zero for months is a lasting warm anomaly, not weather. Then switch to sea-level pressure anomaly at the same point: that line jumps up and down every few days. The ocean remembers; the air doesn't.

How it's made. Every day the build fetches that day's 00 UTC analysis from NOAA's GFS archive on AWS, only the fields shown (about 5.4 MB with the wave file, from byte ranges of the 145 MB file), and keeps them at 1° (about 110 km: enough for patterns this size: about 210 KB a day, 76 MB for the year). Anomalies subtract the same 1991–2020 climatology as the forecast's anomalies, at 00 UTC. Days are never blended: Play steps from one real day to the next. A day that couldn't be fetched is simply missing (the note says how many), and the next build tries again. The waves come from GFS-Wave's analysis for the same day, from the same archive. Observed sea temperature is NOAA's OISST daily mean for the date (0.25°, from satellites, ships and buoys, about 0.6 MB a day from CoastWatch ERDDAP), bilinear onto the 1° grid; it's published about a day after the models, so the newest day has none until the next build. The near-real-time version is used first, as for the forecast's observed-SST map; for the few days it lacks (1–11 January and three single days in 2026) the final, science-quality version fills in. NOAA releases the final version about two weeks after the preliminary one, so every build swaps it in for days more than 20 days old; the note above the map says when a day is still preliminary. The difference is worth having: measured on 15 March and 20 August 2026, the two agree on average (within 0.03 °C) but differ by 0.13–0.15 °C at a typical point, by more than 0.7–0.9 °C at 1 % of points, and by up to 2–3 °C in places, since the final version has had more observations (ships, buoys, late satellite passes) folded in. The build checks the date that comes back: for a missing day the server quietly answers with the nearest day it has, and that must never be filed as the wrong day. The first build fills in as many days as it can in 20 minutes, newest first; later builds finish the year and then add one day each.

Limits. An analysis is a model's reconstruction, very good where there are observations (and over the ocean the satellites see a lot), weaker in data-poor spots. GFS itself changes over the years (new versions); a year is short enough that this rarely matters here. The sea temperature anomaly subtracts an observed climatology (OISST) from the model's sea temperature, so a steady offset of a few tenths of a degree can be the model's own bias rather than the ocean: trust changes through the year more than a small year-round offset. For the ocean itself use sea temperature (observed, OISST): observed minus an observed climatology, with no model in between. The two disagree by enough to matter. On 15 January 2026 the observed anomaly averaged +0.42 °C over the map and the model's −0.02 °C (the model's sea runs about 0.4 °C cool here), and in the Gulf of Alaska the model's year averaged −0.5 °C against an observed 0.0 °C. A small year-round offset in the model's anomaly is its bias, not the ocean. 00 UTC is one moment of the day: late afternoon in California (4 or 5 pm), 2 pm in Hawaiʻi, 9 am in Japan, so 2 m temperatures show a different time of day in different places. The same goes for the wind: a day's map is its 00 UTC moment, not its strongest wind, so a gale that peaked at noon can be missing; particles drift through that one moment's wind, not through a day of real motion.

Simulate: build the circulation from sunlight

The third mode, Simulate (beside Forecast and Past year), is not a forecast and not data: it's a small model of a whole planet's atmosphere that runs live in your browser. You change the planet (how fast it spins, how far its axis tilts, the season, how strongly the Sun heats the equator more than the poles, how much heat the surface stores) and watch the circulation reorganise. The idea (from docs/atmospheric_circulation_reference.md) is to build Earth's circulation up one ingredient at a time, so you can see why the real one looks the way it does.

What the model is. This first stage averages the atmosphere round each latitude circle, so everything depends only on latitude and height: a grid 2° of latitude by 1 km, from the ground to 16 km. Sunlight, worked out for the latitude and day from the tilt (less what the brighter polar ice and cloud reflect), sets a temperature the air is pulled towards over about 10 days; warm air rises and cold air sinks, which drives a north–south and up–down overturning; rotation turns that motion east or west (the Coriolis effect, done by carrying each ring of air's spin round the axis, its angular momentum, with it); and friction at the ground slows the surface wind over about a day. It has no land, no moisture (so no clouds, rain or the extra heating from condensation) and no storms (which need the east–west differences this stage averages away). Those come in later stages.

What's on the screen.

  • The globe: a plain planet with no map, because the model has no land yet (tick "Earth's map, for reference only" to see where the continents would be), with latitude and longitude lines and the equator in red.
  • The particles ride the model's winds. Their east–west motion is the model's wind at its true speed; the overturning (north–south and up–down, only a few cm/s up and down) is shown 10, 30 or 100 times faster, the same for both so particles still follow the cells. Heights are stretched ×40, ×100 or ×200. Low particles are pale, high ones orange: rising air turns orange, sinking air turns pale. Each trails a short tail back along its path, so you can read which way it's going in a still picture. Sliders set how many particles there are (1,000 to 40,000) and how fast they travel through the winds (separate from how fast the model's time runs: slower is easier to follow).
  • Views: in Simulate the view bar has two views, Flat map (the whole world from straight above, to about 75°, centred on the equator at the dateline; everything spread out, particles seen from above) and Globe. The forecast area's views (OPC chart, Oblique, Low pass) are hidden here.
  • Particle options: how many, how fast they travel, their size, brightness and tails (0 for dots only, up to 1.5 s of travel for long streaks), and at what height: all heights, near the ground (below 1.5 km: the surface winds, the trades and westerlies, the air spiralling into lows) or jet-stream level (8–12.5 km).
  • The slab (optional, 2-D model only; the particles fill the whole globe by default): all the particles in one band of longitudes (5° to 90° wide), placed at the globe's right-hand edge so you see it side-on, the way the cross-section chart does: air rising over the equator, flowing poleward high up, sinking in the subtropics and returning along the ground. Looking straight down on it you'd see none of the rising and sinking. A particle blown out of one side of the slab by the east–west wind comes back in the other side at the same latitude and height. That's exact, not a trick: the model is the same at every longitude, so it's what a neighbouring particle would do. "Put the slab side-on to me" moves it to the edge of the globe as you now see it. Untick the slab to spread the particles over the whole globe.
  • The Sun for the date: a yellow circle where it's overhead at some time of that day (its latitude, the declination, set by the date and the tilt), the Sun itself with its ray to the point below it, and light blue circles at the edges of 24-hour daylight and polar night (90° minus the declination: the Arctic and Antarctic circles at the solstices, the poles at the equinoxes). The model's sunlight is averaged over each day, so where the Sun is in longitude doesn't matter to it; it's drawn off to one side of the view.
  • The cross-section (bottom): latitude across (90°S to 90°N), height up. Colour is the east–west wind (red westerly, blue easterly, in m/s); arrows show the overturning's direction (their length grows with the square root of its speed, so weak and strong both show); the dashed line is the tropopause; dotted verticals mark where each Hadley cell ends; dots mark the jets; ▲ marks the ITCZ (the strongest rising air).
  • "Now" in the panel: the cells' reach, the jets, the trade winds, the ITCZ and the equator–pole temperature difference at the ground, all measured from the model as it runs.

The controls. Quick set-ups jump to a planet worth comparing (no spin, Earth in March, June or December, fast spin, no tilt). Then: spin (0 to 4× Earth's, and backwards to −1×; its label gives the length of the day), tilt of the axis (0–90°), the date (its label says where the Sun is overhead), or let the seasons run; equator warmer than the poles by (the heating's difference at an equinox; Earth's is about 60 K); surface heat storage (how many days the surface takes to warm or cool: land-like quick, ocean-like slow); and the model's speed and a pause. The model runs in the background (a Web Worker), so the sliders answer at once. After a change it fast-forwards about 50 model days, a second or two, to the new balance (the note at the top says so); with the seasons running it doesn't, as that would race through them. Start from still air puts the air back to rest and doesn't fast-forward, so you can watch the circulation grow.

Lessons

The Lessons menu at the top of the Simulate tab walks through the ideas in steps, like the Cases: each step sets the planet and the view, says what each part of the screen shows, why, and what to look for, and adds a live note measured from the running model. ◀ ▶ move between steps; anything you change afterwards is yours to play with.

  1. From sunlight to a circulation: still air heated by the Sun (watch it start to move, at one day a second), then the settled no-spin planet with one big cell each side.
  2. Add spin: Coriolis, the trades and the jet: Earth's spin, why the cell stops in the subtropics (the poleward air would be moving 130 m/s faster than the ground by 30°), and faster and slower spins.
  3. Tilt and the seasons: June, December, the seasons running, and no tilt.
  4. Land and ocean: heat storage: a quick land-like surface against a slow ocean-like one.
  5. Earth's three cells, and what's still missing: the textbook picture beside the model.

Three switches go with them (and work in free play too):

  • Labels on the model, placed from the running model's own numbers, never fixed: its cells (Hadley, or one big cell with no spin), rising air (the ITCZ), sinking air (the subtropical highs), the trade winds, the jets. Only what the model makes gets a label.
  • Coriolis arrows: in each hemisphere, near the ground at 12° (air returning to the equator) and high up at 15° (air heading poleward), a grey arrow where the air would go without spin and a yellow one along the model's wind there. Near the ground it's turned west (the trades); high up, east (the jet). In the north that's to the right of where it was heading, in the south to the left.
  • Earth's textbook three cells, in grey: the observed long-term average, with the Hadley (0–30°), Ferrel (30–60°) and polar (60–90°) cells as dashed loops on the chart, the arrows showing which way round the air goes, and on the globe the surface belts (ITCZ, subtropical highs, subpolar lows, polar highs) and winds (trades, westerlies, polar easterlies). This model makes the Hadley cell, the ITCZ, the subtropical highs, the trades and the subtropical jet, but not the Ferrel and polar cells, surface westerlies or subpolar lows. Those come from storms, which a model averaged round the planet can't have; a 3-D stage will add them.

Measured on this model: at Earth's settings the surface trades are about 0.7 m/s and the air rises at the ITCZ at only about 0.5 mm/s 3 km up, around a tenth of Earth's. Without water vapour, rising air gets no extra lift from condensation, so the overturning is weak (the particles show it sped up). With the seasons running, the ITCZ swings between about 19°N and 19°S with 10–30 days of heat storage (15° with 90 days), and the winter jet reaches about 100 m/s against about 20 in summer.

Try this.
  1. No rotation. Set rotation to 0: one huge cell in each hemisphere, rising at the equator and sinking at the pole (Hadley's 1735 picture), no jets, and a small equator–pole difference, because the overturning carries heat all the way. In the model: cells reach about 80°, and the difference falls to about 22 K.
  2. Earth's rotation. Back to 1×: the cells shrink to about 26° (the real Hadley cell reaches about 30°), the air flowing poleward aloft spins up into a westerly jet (37 m/s near 23° in this model), and the returning surface air becomes the easterly trades.
  3. Faster. At 2× the cells end near 20° and the jet weakens to about 17 m/s; at 4×, about 14° and 9 m/s. Fast-spinning planets (Jupiter) have many narrow bands. Spin it backwards (−1×) and everything mirrors: the jets blow from the east and the trades from the west, because "east" is the way the ground turns.
  4. Solstice. Set day 172 (21 June): the winter hemisphere's cell grows, crosses the equator and dominates; the ITCZ moves into the summer hemisphere (about 17°N here); the winter jet is several times the summer one. This is why the real ITCZ migrates with the seasons, and why the southern winter's jet is so strong.

The 3-D model: storms, and the cells they make

The Model menu switches to a second, much bigger model: a 3-D global atmosphere of the kind climate scientists use to test their models' cores (Held & Suarez 1994). It solves the full equations of motion on a 64 × 32 grid round the whole globe, at 6 levels up to about 20 km, with no moisture and no land yet, heated by the same sunlight as the 2-D model (so spin, tilt, season and heating work the same). Unlike the 2-D model, every longitude is free to differ, so it makes storms: the jet from lesson 2 is unstable, and its ripples grow into travelling lows and highs. It runs in the background at about one model day a second, and starts from a state spun up for 330 model days (Start from still air watches the storms grow from nothing instead, in about two weeks of model time).

  • Colour on the globe: temperature near the ground (σ 0.92, about 0.6 km) or at mid-levels (about 4 km), or surface pressure (blue low, red high). Fronts show as bands where the colours crowd, wrapped round the lows.
  • Labels: the lows (L) and highs (H), found as local extremes of the model's surface pressure outside the tropics.
  • Flow lines of the wind near the ground (the lowest level, about 0.6 km): lines traced along the wind from seeds every 9° or so, each about 2,000 km long, faint where it starts and bright where it's heading, yellower where the wind is stronger. Redrawn about once a second. They show the surface circulation at a glance: the spirals into lows (counter-clockwise in the north), out of highs, the trades converging on the equator.
  • Particles: the winds at their true speed, horizontally; the rising and sinking sped up (the same menu as before). The slab and the Coriolis arrows are 2-D tools and are hidden here: with storms every longitude differs.
  • The chart: the zonal-mean wind in colour and the overturning as lines (every 2 × 10¹⁰ kg/s; solid and dashed turn opposite ways), either right now or averaged over the last 30 days. Any one day is dominated by storms; the average shows the cells, labelled from the model with their strengths.

Why it matters. Averaged over 100 model days, this model has easterly trades up to 5 m/s at 3–30°, westerlies at 36–64° (9 m/s at 53°) and weak polar easterlies at the ground; a Hadley cell to about 25° (7 × 10¹⁰ kg/s); and beyond it a Ferrel cell turning the other way (3 × 10¹⁰ kg/s at 36–60°). Nothing in the model drives that cell directly. It is what the storms' carrying of heat poleward and of westerly momentum into the jet look like when averaged round the planet: the storms make the cell, not the other way round. The polar cell is very weak, as it is in the benchmark. Lesson 6 walks through it.

Continents (the 3-D model)

Tick Earth's continents (3-D model) and the model gets land where Earth has it: Natural Earth's coastlines (the same as the basemaps) sampled on a 1° grid and averaged into the model's grid boxes (29 % of the area, as on Earth). They're drawn on the ground as the model sees them, tinted with a dark coastline. Land differs from sea in two ways here:

  • Heat storage. The surface temperature the air is pulled toward follows the Sun within about 5 days over land, but takes about 100 days over the sea (an upper ocean stirs its heat down through tens of metres of water). Over land the air near the ground is also tied to the surface at every latitude.
  • Roughness. Friction near the ground is twice as strong over land as over sea.

With the seasons running, that's enough for the continents to make their own weather patterns. Measured over 30 days in the model, 40–60°N:

  • July: a thermal low over hot Asia (991 hPa against 1002 over the North Pacific; its lowest-level air 6 K warmer), reaching to South Asia (995 hPa), the dry skeleton of the monsoon low.
  • January: high pressure over cold Asia (1010 hPa against 1000 over the North Pacific: a Siberian high) and lows over the northern oceans (995 hPa in the North Atlantic: an Icelandic low; the North Pacific's is the Aleutian low).

Earth's contrasts are much bigger (the Siberian high is about 35 hPa above the Aleutian low; Siberia at −30 °C against about +5 °C over the North Pacific in January). This model has no snow reflecting sunlight, no clear dry air cooling to space, and no rain heating the summer monsoon, all of which strengthen them; and no mountains yet. Lesson 7 walks through July and January, starting from states spun up with the continents and seasons over 16 model months.

Its limits. It's dry (no clouds or rain, so the tropics are too calm and the trades too weak compared with Earth), has no mountains yet, and a 64 × 32 grid (about 600 km) keeps storms broad and smooth.

How it compares with Earth. The pattern is right, the size is not quite: without moisture the rising air in the tropics gets no extra lift from condensation, so the overturning is weaker than Earth's, and without storms nothing carries heat poleward beyond the cells, so the jets sit closer to the equator. Real mid-latitudes have a second, eddy-driven circulation (the Ferrel cell) that this stage can't make; the next stages add the east–west dimension, storms, land and the ocean's patterns (El Niño and La Niña).

Lesson outlines

Each outline is a starting point: a goal, the settings, what to do, what students should notice, and questions to ask. They use whatever the weather is on the day, so the particulars differ; the ideas don't. Times are rough.

1. Pressure levels are surfaces in the air (20 min)

  • Goal: a "500 mb chart" is a map of the height of a surface, and its highs and lows are hills and valleys.
  • Set-up: "What steers the storms" (500 mb by height, relief ×5), Oblique view.
  • Do: set relief to ×1, then back to ×5; switch to the OPC chart view (straight down); turn the contours on and off. Pin the probe in a trough and on a ridge and compare the 500 mb heights.
  • Notice: from above, the contours look exactly like a chart; from the side, they're a bumpy sheet. Troughs are low because the air beneath is cold (thin).
  • Ask: why is 500 mb lower over Alaska than over Hawaiʻi? (Colder air below; see thickness.) What does relief ×5 exaggerate, and what does it leave true?

2. Anatomy of a mid-latitude storm (30 min)

  • Goal: a storm is a 3-D structure: a surface low, a trough aloft to its west, a jet above, fronts and a comma cloud.
  • Set-up: "Where's the weather now?", then "A storm in 3-D".
  • Do: find the comma cloud and its L; add 500 and 250 mb as context; slice across the cold front; play 12–24 h.
  • Notice: the 500 mb trough lags west of the surface low while it deepens; the jet streak sits over the storm's poleward side; the θ lines slope across the front.
  • Ask: where would you expect the heaviest rain? Is the storm still deepening, and how can you tell from the levels' alignment? Is it a "bomb"? (Recipe: follow one storm.)

3. Why forecasts disagree (20 min)

  • Goal: forecasts are uncertain in a way that grows with time, and comparing models shows it.
  • Set-up: "GFS vs ECMWF"; pin a probe at a coastal city; tick both models. Then colour the surface by sea-level pressure as GFS − ECMWF.
  • Do: play; flip M at day 1, 3, 5, 7.
  • Notice: day 1–2 nearly identical; small differences grow; by day 5–7 a low may be in a different place or not exist in one model.
  • Ask: if the two disagree, which is right? (Neither can be known yet: the disagreement is the information.) What would a forecaster say to a sailor planning a passage on day 6?

4. Ocean and atmosphere (20 min)

  • Goal: sea temperature shapes the weather, and the weather drives the sea.
  • Set-up: "Warm and cold water (observed)"; then the surface's own sea temperature fill.
  • Do: find the warm pool, the cold tongue, the Kuroshio edge; check Niño-3.4 by hovering along the equator 170°W–120°W; overlay the 10 m wind particles.
  • Notice: the trades blow toward the warm pool; storms track along the Kuroshio's edge; anomalies pick out the unusual patches.
  • Ask: why is the east Pacific equator cold, when the sun is overhead? (Upwelling.) What would El Niño change?

5. Tropics and mid-latitudes (20 min)

  • Goal: the two regimes work differently: steady trades and thunderstorms vs. travelling storms along a jet.
  • Set-up: "Trade winds and the ITCZ", Whole view, satellite on.
  • Do: compare the particles north and south of 20°N; slice north–south along 180°; find any typhoon.
  • Notice: near the equator the contours are flat and the wind doesn't follow them; the tropopause is much higher over the tropics; a typhoon has no front and no tilt.
  • Ask: why do the height contours stop explaining the wind near the equator? (The Coriolis effect vanishes; see contours and wind.)

6. A storm in a box (30 min)

  • Goal: a mid-latitude storm is a 3-D machine: air spirals in low down, climbs, and spreads out aloft, and the spin, rising and fronts are all parts of it.
  • Set-up: follow the walk-through to a box round the deepest mid-latitude low, 1–7 km, light background, map on the surface.
  • Do: quantity vorticity (shape at 8), then divergence (shape at 4), then rising/sinking air, each with 3-D flow lines (rise and sink ×5). Orbit the box; press From below; step the time 12 h.
  • Notice: the red spin column leans west with height; converging air low down and spreading air aloft bracket the rising air; the flow lines climb on the east side and sink on the west.
  • Ask: where is the cloud and rain, and how could you tell without the satellite? Is the storm deepening (is the spin column leaning or upright)? Then turn the satellite on and check.

7. The jet stream, its streaks and turbulence (20 min)

  • Goal: the jet is a 3-D tube of fast air; its streaks drive rising air, and its shear makes turbulence.
  • Set-up: "The jet stream"; then the 3-D tab, a layer 6–12.5 km, wind speed as a shape at 100 kt.
  • Do: orbit the tube; switch to turbulence risk (shape at 8), then divergence; slice across the tube with a wall and Plane.
  • Notice: the tube is only 3–5 km deep but thousands of km long; turbulence sheets hug its top and bottom; red divergence at a streak's left exit.
  • Ask: a flight from Tokyo to Seattle: where would you fly to ride the jet, and where would you expect bumps? Why do eastbound flights take less time than westbound?

8. Fronts in 3-D (25 min)

  • Goal: a front is a sloping boundary between air masses, not a line on the ground.
  • Set-up: Layers, 850 mb coloured by thermal gradient; then the recipe see a front in 3-D.
  • Do: thermal gradient as a shape at 1.2 in a box on the front; Edge-on; then θe everywhere; then frontogenesis. Lay OPC's surface chart under it.
  • Notice: the sheet leans back over the cold air; θe changes sharply across it; the forecasters' drawn front lies along the sheet's bottom edge.
  • Ask: if the front slopes, where does its cloud sit relative to the surface front, ahead or behind? Why do warm fronts bring long steady rain and cold fronts short showers?

9. The lid on the weather (15 min)

  • Goal: the tropopause caps the weather, and it's much higher in the tropics.
  • Set-up: the recipe find the tropopause: a north–south wall along 180°, quantity stability, Plane on.
  • Do: read the height of the jump at 60°N, 40°N and 10°N; then fill stability as a shape at 8 over the whole atmosphere and look from the side.
  • Notice: the step drops from the tropics to the pole, with a break under the jet; a thin stable layer low down over the subtropics (the trade-wind inversion).
  • Ask: why are tropical thunderstorms taller than those at 50°N? What would a thunderstorm's anvil do when it hits the lid?

Teaching with the cases

  • Each case is a ready-made lesson of 10–20 minutes: open it on a projector and press Next, pausing at each Ask: for discussion before revealing the answer in brackets.
  • Pair cases that teach the same idea: the June heat dome and January's stuck ridge (a ridge in summer bakes the ground; in winter it leaves a warm lid over cold valleys); the December atmospheric rivers and the Kona lows (moisture plus lift makes rain); January's stuck ridge and Hawaiʻi's giant swell (a blocked Northwest sends the storm track, and its waves, toward Hawaiʻi); La Niña to El Niño and Hawaiʻi's 2026 hurricanes (the ocean sets up the season).
  • Note the time: each analysis is 00 UTC, which is the afternoon of the previous day in the Northwest (5 pm PDT, 4 pm PST) and in Hawaiʻi (2 pm). The cases say so where it matters.
  • After a case, give students the date and a question, and let them find the answer in the Past year themselves.

Part IV — How it's built

The daily build

A Python program (src/build_site.py) runs once a day and writes a static website; there's no server doing work when you view it. The build time, B, is taken once at the start and used by every step.

  1. Find each model's newest complete run (find_latest_run). For each 6-hourly run, newest first, it asks the servers whether every forecast step is posted: one HEAD request per step for the step's index file, AWS first, then the producer's server. The clock alone isn't trusted, because posting takes hours and varies. A run missing any step is skipped. So ECMWF's 06Z/18Z runs, which stop at 144 h, never qualify; they're still used for past hours.
  2. Build the satellite frames for [B − 24 h, B], every 20 minutes (render_satellite_frames). Frames are built in parallel processes sized to the machine (up to 6, about 3 GB of memory each; the environment variables NP3D_SAT_WORKERS and NP3D_SAT_THREADS override it on a given computer). The lookup that maps each satellite's disk onto our map is built once and reused: the first frame that has both satellites is built alone and writes it, then the rest go in parallel (several processes writing it at once would collide). Measured: about 4–5 s per frame in parallel, limited by downloading ~48 MB of full-disk data per frame, so about 5–6 minutes a day.
  3. Fetch OPC's charts, the climatology (first build only) and the newest observed sea temperature. Each is optional: if one fails, the page simply doesn't offer it.
  4. Export the model data (export_atmos3d): plan which run supplies each valid time (plan_timeline), download each step, cut it to the domain, pack it, and write the manifest.
  5. Write the page and this guide (atmos3d_page).

Retention. Nothing piles up: raw model and satellite downloads are deleted as each step finishes and again at the start of every build (in case one was interrupted); each build's satellite frames are kept for 2 builds; the site is rebuilt from scratch every time. The published copy is a single commit, replaced daily.

Every step is isolated: a failed download is listed as missing and the rest carry on. The program always finishes. If nothing could be built, the page says so and the previous published site stays up. A time budget stops downloads stuck in retries.

Where and when. The build runs on a Mac at home, early each morning Pacific time (about 02:00), after both models' 00 UTC runs have posted. A script (scripts/run_daily.sh) first fetches the newest version of the code, builds, and publishes only if the build produced model data, so a bad morning leaves yesterday's site up rather than an empty one. The file build-info.json next to the page records which version of the code built it and when. The tests run after publishing, as a health check.

From model files to the page

Which run supplies each time

One rule for the whole timeline, per model: the newest 6-hourly run that started at or before the valid time, but no newer than the model's newest complete run. So past hours get an earlier run's +0 h or +3 h, and future hours get the newest run. The timeline starts at B − 24 h (rounded down to 3 h) and ends at each model's start + 168 h. Steps are every 3 h to +144 h, then every 6 h. The two models are lined up by valid time; display time zero is the GFS run's start.

Cutting to the domain

Each variable is read from the GRIB file separately, then points are picked exactly from the model's own grid at 0.5° spacing, never interpolated. Both GFS's 0.5° and ECMWF's 0.25° grids contain every 0.5° point, so ECMWF is subsampled, not smoothed. The data area runs 20°S–65°N, 135°E–115°W across the dateline: 171 × 221 points. Raw files are deleted as soon as each step is cut.

Packing

Each step is stored as small gzipped files ("bundles"), one for the surface and one per level. Values are 16-bit integers at a fixed resolution per quantity: value = offset + code × resolution, with code 65535 meaning missing. The resolutions are in parameters. They're fixed rather than scaled to each file's range, so neighbouring values repeat and compress well, and they're finer than anything the page displays. Files are named by valid time (YYYYMMDDHH). A manifest lists every file, the run and lead behind each step, the grid, the projection and anything missing.

Sea surface temperature

Neither model's open output has a plain "SST" field on this schedule. What they give is the temperature of the surface itself: GFS's surface temperature (its sea-surface scheme, over water) and ECMWF's skin temperature. Over open water, that is the sea surface temperature, within a few tenths of a degree of the water just below in strong sun. So the layer keeps it over open sea only: land (land fraction ≥ 0.5 in each model's own land-sea mask) and sea ice (GFS ice cover ≥ 15 %, the usual ice edge; ECMWF ice thickness ≥ 1 cm) are left missing and drawn see-through, never filled in. It's the models' sea temperature, not an observation. The ocean changes slowly, so it barely moves over 7 days.

Past-year archive

For each day: 16 messages of the atmosphere file and 3 of the wave file (about 5.4 MB together) fetched (four days at a time), points picked exactly from the 0.5° grid onto a 1° grid over the same box (86 × 111), sea temperature masked to open sea like the forecast's, anomalies from the climatology at 00 UTC (reanalysis fields bilinear from 2.5°, sea temperature from OISST's daily climatology), packed like the forecast files and kept in data/archive/ between builds. A day that fails is tried once more at once (a download can come back short), then left for the next build. Days older than 365 are deleted, except those in an event window.

Event archive

Some days are kept for good: the event windows in config/atmos3d.yaml (archive.events). They hold the days the Cases tab needs, so a case never rolls out with the year, and famous events of earlier years that the rolling year never had: the June 2021 heat dome, the October 2021 bomb cyclone, the November 2024 bomb cyclone. They're the same GFS 00 UTC analysis as the Past year, made the same way (NOAA's archive of GFS runs on AWS starts in 2021), with observed sea temperature from the final OISST. ECMWF's free archive doesn't reach back, so events are GFS only, like the whole Past year. Each build fetches any window day it lacks, after the rolling year's, within the same time budget; about 0.23 MB a day. A test fails if a case uses a day that no window covers.

A case step on an event day shows that event's window in the Past year viewer: the day slider covers only those days, and the day bar and notes above the map say "event archive". Closing the case goes back to the rolling year.

Climatology and anomalies

The first build downloads the 1991–2020 averages (see anomalies), fits each grid point's annual cycle with four harmonics (per UTC hour for the reanalysis) and keeps only those coefficients in data/clim/; later builds reuse them. Each build then writes the average for every 00/06/12/18 UTC on the timeline at the reanalysis's own 2.5° (about 40 KB each: the average is that smooth anyway), the daily sea-temperature average for each date at 0.5°, and the newest observed OISST day with its anomaly. The anomaly itself is computed in the browser: the model step minus the average, interpolated to the step's grid and hour.

OPC charts

For each product and lead, the issue after the newest 00Z/12Z cycle is downloaded (one cycle earlier if nothing is posted yet). Its valid time is read off the chart itself by OCR of the printed "VALID" stamp: file names carry the issue time, not the valid time. A chart whose stamp can't be read is left out, never guessed. Each chart is cropped by a rectangle measured from its own lat/lon gridlines (fitted linear in longitude and true Mercator in latitude) to exactly the OPC box, 16–65°N, 135°E–115°W. Measured against our coastline on the real charts, the best alignment is within one pixel (≈9 km). The downloader and OCR come from the OPC gallery project (opc-sa-reconstruct).

Vertical motion

ω (Pa/s, + down) at each level becomes w = −ω R T / (p g) in cm/s, + up, with the level's own temperature (dry air; using T in place of the virtual temperature errs by under 1 %). Stored at 0.1 cm/s in the level's file. A step whose file lacks ω keeps everything else and has no vertical motion. It adds about a sixth to each GFS step's download (9 → 12 MB).

Waves

For every step, after the atmosphere, the model's own wave file for the same run and lead is fetched (about 2–3 MB) and its 0.5° points are picked exactly from the 0.25° grid, like the atmosphere. Land and sea ice are missing and drawn see-through. Stored as a separate "wave" file per step: height to 1 cm, period to 0.01 s, direction to 0.1°. If the waves of a step can't be fetched, that step keeps its atmosphere and the page notes the gap.

OPC wind & wave charts

Laid out differently from OPC's other charts (140°E–120°W, 20–62°N, with the wave-height legend inside the map), so their position comes from their own gridlines: true Mercator (the parallels' spacing follows the Mercator formula), fitted to the 10° meridians, and checked against our coastline to within one pixel (≈12 km).

HFO charts

HFO's files have fixed names ("the latest 18Z analysis"), so the date comes only from the chart: its VALID line is read by OCR and accepted only if it's consistent with the printed ISSUED time (an analysis is issued a few hours after its time, a forecast before) and the ISSUED time with the file's own date on the server. Otherwise the chart is left out. Both of HFO's chart types turned out to be true Mercator (the spacing of their 10° gridlines follows the Mercator formula exactly), like our map, so the part inside our data area is one exact rectangle: fitted to the gridlines to within 1.5 pixels, and measured against our coastline to within 1–2 display pixels (8–17 km), about one of HFO's own pixels.

Below ground

Where the surface pressure is lower than a level's pressure (over high land), that level lies underground: it isn't drawn there, and particles aren't placed there.

Satellite frames

Target times are every 20 minutes, ending at B rounded down to the interval. For each one, Himawari's scan from the 10-minute slot at or before it is used (they start on the slot, so it matches), along with the GOES scan starting closest to it, within 15 minutes. GOES scans start about every 10 minutes, so normally within a minute of the target. Frames are labelled with the target time; recording each scan's own start time is planned. It's resampled onto the map's Mercator grid (1000 × 966 px) with Satpy's nearest-neighbour resampling, and the two are joined at 181.75°E. That's halfway between the sub-satellite points, where both view angles are equal. Measured on real frames, the GOES−Himawari brightness difference crosses zero at 181.1–183.2°E, and is 0.01–0.45 K at the seam. Brightness temperature is mapped to grey: 190 K white to 300 K black. A time with either satellite missing is skipped, never filled. Himawari has no full-disk scan at 02:40 and 14:40 UTC (daily housekeeping).

Map and projection

WGS84 ellipsoidal Mercator centred on 180°, in km. The browser's own Mercator is checked on load against points the build computes with pyproj, and must agree to well under 1 km (it agrees to about 0.001 km). The globe is a sphere of the Earth's mean radius (6,371 km), turned so the map's centre touches the flat map's tangent plane; switching views blends between the two positions. The basemap is drawn with cartopy at 4096 px wide (≈3 km per pixel).

Assumptions and limitations

  • A model is not the atmosphere. Even the past hours are model analyses, very good but not observations.
  • 0.5° smooths. Tropical cyclones, small lows and sharp fronts are broader and weaker than in reality.
  • Blending across 3 or 6 hours can ghost fast-moving features; it's labelled, and readouts snap to real steps.
  • Heights are geopotential heights, within about 0.5 % of true height at these levels.
  • Mercator stretches high latitudes: at 60°N distances are drawn twice as long as at the equator. The globe view shows true proportions.
  • Satellite IR sees cloud tops, not cloud bases; low cloud is hard to see; images near the seam and near the edges of each satellite's view are slightly less reliable.
  • The two models' start times can differ (ECMWF may be one run older when its newest run isn't posted in time).
  • "Now" is your device's clock; the data is from the last build.
  • Anomalies are measured against a coarse (2.5°) reanalysis made with a 1990s model. Part of any anomaly is the difference between that model and today's, largest near mountains and steep coasts. At 03/09/15/21 UTC the average is interpolated in time, missing up to ~1 hPa of the tropical pressure tide.
  • Observed sea temperature is one day's blended analysis (OISST), about a day behind, at 0.25° smoothed to 0.5°. Coastal points touching land are left out.
  • OPC's charts are images: their valid time is read by OCR (never guessed; unreadable charts are left out), and their position depends on a crop measured once from their gridlines (checked to within about one pixel, 9 km). If OPC changes its chart layout, the crop must be re-measured.

What's real on the screen

  • Every field is from a real model run, named on screen, at its real time. Nothing is made up or filled in.
  • Between two real model steps the picture is blended, and labelled "interpolated". Hover readouts, barbs and live notes always use the nearer real step.
  • The two models are never blended together. A difference between them, when shown, is its own labelled layer.
  • Satellite frames are real scans at their real times, never blended.
  • OPC's charts are OPC's published images, shown only near the valid time printed on them, with their office named.
  • Anomalies are always labelled with their baseline (1991–2020) and source; the observed sea temperature with its date, because it doesn't change with the slider.
  • The page shows when it was built, each model's run, the last satellite frame, and now.
  • Every data file is fetched with this build's id, so a browser can't show a cached file from an earlier build under this build's labels (it happened once during development; see the changelog).

How it was checked

Everything was checked against real data, not only tests on made-up numbers. In summary:

  • For every step, the run's start plus its lead equals the step's valid time.
  • The browser's projection agrees with pyproj to 0.0006 km, and the satellite images line up with the basemap to 0 km.
  • Coastlines on the basemap are within one pixel (3 km).
  • There's no seam at the dateline in the fields or the satellite.
  • The OPC box corners are exact.
  • Live notes match values recomputed in Python from the raw files, as do the ruler's level heights (to 0.000 m).
  • Slice values and volume cells equal an independent Python calculation (the volume: 42,715 of 1,813,968 cells above 70 kt in both).
  • H and L: the fast search gives identical results to the original on all 180 real fields of a build.
  • OPC's charts: valid times read off the charts match OPC's schedule; the crop lines up with our coastline to 0 px (surface) and 1 px ≈ 9 km (500 mb); the model's lows sit on OPC's (e.g. L 989 in the Gulf of Alaska, H 1030).
  • Anomalies: the tropical pressure anomaly stays steady across 00/06/12/18 UTC (the tide is removed); the observed sea-temperature anomaly is 0.23 °C below OISST's own (1971–2000) one, as the baselines' warming predicts.
  • Layout: panels, probe, colour keys, slice chart and time box are measured on screen at 1280 × 800 and 436 × 858 (no overlaps), not judged by eye.
  • Playback: one frame costs about 4–6 ms across step changes (measured in the browser), with no per-step hitch.

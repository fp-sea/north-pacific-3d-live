Marble. It rolls downhill on the focus level: into the troughs at 500 mb, into the lows at the surface. That's the shape; real air doesn't roll downhill, it runs along the lines, turned by the Earth's spin. Drop a few and watch them gather. More →
Constant-level balloon. It floats on the focus level, like the balloons scientists fly for weeks at one pressure level, drifting with that level's wind and leaving a trail. Press Play and it travels through time with the weather. Try one on each side of the jet, or upwind of a low. (A real weather balloon rises until it bursts: tick fly it as a weather balloon under lift.) More →

Where's this swell from? Click a spot at sea, say off a north shore: the swell arriving there at the time on screen is traced backward at its own speed (longer-period swell travels faster: 14 s swell about 21 kt, 940 km a day) along the great circle it came on (in deep water swell keeps to great circles), with a dot every day; the wave model gives the seas at each point at its own earlier time. The red ball marks where the seas were biggest on the way: most likely the storm that made it, often days earlier and thousands of km away. Swell from the far side of the Pacific can take a week; move the time forward to trace further back (trace back sets how far). More →
Smoke. A source that lets go of a puff every model hour, each carried by the wind: think of Kīlauea's vog or a wildfire. Press Play: a steady wind draws a thin plume; a turning wind bends it; in 3-D, wind changing with height tears it apart. With turbulent spread on, the plume widens as real smoke does, and in 3-D a surface source stays trapped under the boundary layer's top. (On a mountain island the global model's smooth terrain lifts it over the top; the real trades mostly go around.) More →
Lift a parcel. Click a spot: a parcel of its surface air is lifted through the model's column. It cools as it rises (9.8 °C per km) until its water vapour condenses, its cloud base; above that it cools more slowly, warmed by the condensing. Where it's warmer than the air around it, it rises by itself; where colder, it stops. Over the trades, it usually stops under the trade inversion (1.5–2.5 km): flat-topped cumulus, and the lid that squeezes wind through the channels. Try a Kona low or a front for a deep, buoyant column, and warm the surface air to see the afternoon sun build showers. Press Play: it follows the changing column. Tick fly it as a weather balloon to launch a radiosonde instead: it drifts with the wind as it rises (about 35 minutes to the top of the data), reads each level on the way and adds the winds. More →
Forces on the air. Click a spot: arrows show what pushes the air there. Red: the pressure push, from high toward low, stronger where the lines are close. Blue: Earth's spin, at right angles to the wind (to its right in the north). Grey: what's left: friction at the surface. White: the wind; yellow: the geostrophic wind, where push and spin balance. Aloft the wind runs nearly along the height lines (push and spin balance); at the surface, friction slows it and turns it across the isobars toward the low. Switch the focus level (Layers) to compare. More →
Ring of balloons. 24 balloons in a circle. Press Play: near a front it stretches long and thin (deformation); drawn into a low its area shrinks (the air converges and must rise); under a high it spreads out (the air diverges as it sinks). The label gives its area against the start. More →

A model planet, heated by the Sun, to see why Earth's winds are arranged as they are, one ingredient at a time. Not the real weather (that's the Forecast mode).

All settings

The 2-D model treats every longitude alike, so it has only latitude and height (90 × 16 boxes): it settles quickly and shows the Hadley cell, jet and trades cleanly, but it can never have storms. The 3-D model solves the same equations weather models use, on a 64 × 32 grid with 6 levels, so the jet breaks into storms by itself; it's slower and noisier, and its circulation shows best in 30-day averages.

Labels are placed from the model's own numbers as it runs (only what it actually makes gets one); the Coriolis arrows compare where moving air would go without spin (grey) with where the model's air goes (yellow); the textbook cells are Earth's observed long-term average, drawn as a schematic to compare with.

Quick set-ups

The planet

Spin turns moving air sideways (the Coriolis effect): to the right in the north, the left in the south. Without it, air flows straight from the hot equator to the cold poles.

Tilt makes seasons: over a year the Sun's overhead point moves between the tilt's latitude north and south.

The date sets where the Sun is overhead (its declination) and so which latitudes get the most daily sunlight; held fixed, the model settles to that day's balance ("perpetual" June, say). With the seasons running, the date advances with model time and the circulation chases the Sun, lagging by the surface's heat storage. The sunlight is a daily average: there's no day and night.

The sea doesn't move in this model: its temperatures are the observed average (NOAA ERSST v5), plus an observed event's difference from normal. Dry, the sea's east–west pattern is doubled in the heating and a stand-in heats the air over warm water (both labelled boosts for the missing heat of rain). Water vapour replaces both with modelled rain, counts the pattern once, and also makes the model's radiation relax faster (15 days instead of 40), because the rain's heat must be radiated away: compare dry and moist at the same radiation (below). Changing any of these fast-forwards 40 days to the new balance.

How quickly the free atmosphere is pulled back toward its sunlight-set temperature (Held & Suarez's 40 days). Shorter: a stronger pull, a sharper equator–pole contrast and stronger jets. Set it to 15 to compare dry with moist fairly.

The Sun's heating

The temperature difference the sunlight alone would make. More difference, stronger winds.

How long the surface takes to warm up or cool down. Oceans store heat, so their seasons come later and are milder; this matters once the seasons run. (With continents on, the 3-D model uses 5 days for land and 100 for the sea instead.)

How quickly each layer of air is pulled toward its sunlight-set temperature. Slow radiation gives the circulation longer to carry heat, so the equator–pole difference shrinks.

How quickly friction slows the wind in the lowest 1.5 km. Weaker friction (a longer time) lets the surface winds, the trades, get stronger.

What you see

How fast the particles travel through the winds (separate from how fast the model's time runs). Slower is easier to follow; faster shows the paths.

A tail is a short line back along the particle's path: longer tails show the flow's shape; none shows just the dots.

Which slice the chart shows. The ⓘ key explains how to read whichever is on.

The overturning is slow (in this model air rises millimetres a second; Earth's average ITCZ ascent is about half a centimetre a second), so the particles show it faster. The east–west wind is shown at its true speed. Heights are stretched because the atmosphere is very thin: 16 km on a planet 12,700 km across.

Running

After you change a setting the model fast-forwards to the new balance (the 2-D model about 200 model days in about 6 seconds; the 3-D model 40 days in about 10 seconds), then the 3-D model averages 30 days afresh. "Start from still air" doesn't, so you can watch the circulation grow.

How to read a case

  • Each step sets the map to one real day of a weather model's analysis (the time is just above the step's title). ◀ ▶ move between steps (in Present mode, the arrow keys too); ▶ Play the days runs the step's days on the map.
  • Read the story, then Look for: what to find on the map. The Takeaway is the step's point in one line.
  • Ask is a question to think about. Quiz me hides the answer until you open it; just teach shows it.
  • Words with a dotted underline have a short definition: tap or click them.
  • The folded sections go deeper: the map key (what the colours and lines mean), the hour-by-hour high-resolution map with model and observed views, what the stations measured, and the forecasters' numbers.
  • The map is yours at any step: turn it, zoom, and hover or tap for values. The last step sums the case up and asks one question across it.

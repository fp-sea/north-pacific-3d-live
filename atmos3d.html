Marble. It rolls downhill on the focus level: into the troughs at 500 mb, into the lows at the surface. That's the shape; real air doesn't roll downhill, it runs along the lines, turned by the Earth's spin. Drop a few and watch them gather. More →
Constant-level balloon. It floats on the focus level, like the balloons scientists fly for weeks at one pressure level, drifting with that level's wind and leaving a trail. Press Play and it travels through time with the weather. Try one on each side of the jet, or upwind of a low. (A real weather balloon rises until it bursts: tick fly it as a weather balloon under lift.) More →
Air parcel. A bit of the air itself: carried by the wind at its own height, it also rises and sinks with the model's vertical motion, starting at the focus level's height. Drop one beside a balloon and press Play: ahead of a trough the parcel climbs into faster wind, under a ridge it sinks, and the two part. Its label gives its height and ↑ rising, ↓ sinking. More →
Where did this air come from? Click a spot: the blue line is the path the air arriving there took, traced backward hour by hour, a dot every day. Is the moist air over Hawaiʻi from the tropics? Did that cold air come off Siberia? In 3-D, see whether it came down from aloft (sinking air dries and warms) or up from the surface. Move the time forward first to trace further back. Choose arrive at three heights to trace air arriving 500 m, 1.5 km and 5.5 km up at once; the chart above shows each path's height, temperature and humidity on the way. More →
Where's this swell from? Click a spot at sea, say off a north shore: the swell arriving there at the time on screen is traced backward at its own speed (longer-period swell travels faster: 14 s swell about 21 kt, 940 km a day) along the great circle it came on (in deep water swell keeps to great circles), with a dot every day; the wave model gives the seas at each point at its own earlier time. The red ball marks where the seas were biggest on the way: most likely the storm that made it, often days earlier and thousands of km away. Swell from the far side of the Pacific can take a week; move the time forward to trace further back (trace back sets how far). More →
Smoke. A source that lets go of a puff every model hour, each carried by the wind: think of Kīlauea's vog or a wildfire. Press Play: a steady wind draws a thin plume; a turning wind bends it; in 3-D, wind changing with height tears it apart. With turbulent spread on, the plume widens as real smoke does, and in 3-D a surface source stays trapped under the boundary layer's top. (On a mountain island the global model's smooth terrain lifts it over the top; the real trades mostly go around.) More →
Lift a parcel. Click a spot: a parcel of its surface air is lifted through the model's column. It cools as it rises (9.8 °C per km) until its water vapour condenses, its cloud base; above that it cools more slowly, warmed by the condensing. Where it's warmer than the air around it, it rises by itself; where colder, it stops. Over the trades, it usually stops under the trade inversion (1.5–2.5 km): flat-topped cumulus, and the lid that squeezes wind through the channels. Try a Kona low or a front for a deep, buoyant column, and warm the surface air to see the afternoon sun build showers. Press Play: it follows the changing column. Tick fly it as a weather balloon to launch a radiosonde instead: it drifts with the wind as it rises (about 35 minutes to the top of the data), reads each level on the way and adds the winds. More →
Forces on the air. Click a spot: arrows show what pushes the air there. Red: the pressure push, from high toward low, stronger where the lines are close. Blue: Earth's spin, at right angles to the wind (to its right in the north). Grey: what's left: friction at the surface. White: the wind; yellow: the geostrophic wind, where push and spin balance. Aloft the wind runs nearly along the height lines (push and spin balance); at the surface, friction slows it and turns it across the isobars toward the low. Switch the focus level (Layers) to compare. More →
Ring of balloons. 24 balloons in a circle. Press Play: near a front it stretches long and thin (deformation); drawn into a low its area shrinks (the air converges and must rise); under a high it spreads out (the air diverges as it sinks). The label gives its area against the start. More →

A small model of a planet's atmosphere, heated only by the Sun. Change the planet and watch the winds reorganise. It isn't the real weather (that's the Forecast mode), and it has no land, storms or clouds yet.

Lessons

Quick set-ups

The planet

Spin turns moving air sideways (the Coriolis effect): to the right in the north, the left in the south. Without it, air flows straight from the hot equator to the cold poles.

Tilt makes seasons: over a year the Sun's overhead point moves between the tilt's latitude north and south.

The Sun's heating

The temperature difference the sunlight alone would make. More difference, stronger winds.

How long the surface takes to warm up or cool down. Oceans store heat, so their seasons come later and are milder; this matters once the seasons run.

What you see

How fast the particles travel through the winds (separate from how fast the model's time runs). Slower is easier to follow; faster shows the paths.

A tail is a short line back along the particle's path: longer tails show the flow's shape; none shows just the dots.

The overturning is slow (air rises a few centimetres a second), so the particles show it faster. The east–west wind is shown at its true speed.

Running

After you change a setting the model fast-forwards (about 50 model days in a second or two) to the new balance. "Start from still air" doesn't, so you can watch the circulation grow.

Now

Real North Pacific events of the past year, a step at a time: each step sets up the map and the day, and says what to look for. Use Next and Back; the map is yours to explore at any step.
